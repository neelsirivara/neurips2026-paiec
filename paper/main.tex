\begin{abstract}
The Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC) asks for the probability that an AI system (the \emph{subject}) answers a benchmark \emph{item} correctly, given the subject's visible attributes, the item text, and between 0 and 31 labels acquired on the same subject--benchmark pair. Scoring is the Brier area-under-the-learning-curve (ALC), which places 60\% of its weight on budgets of at most seven labels. We describe a submission built without the official training release (\texttt{measurement-db} is gated and we had no credentials), i.e.\ a predictor with no fitted parameters: a hand-set attribute prior at budget zero, beta-binomial shrinkage of the pair success rate towards that prior, and two item-level adaptation components---a ridge-logistic adjustment on cheap item features and a TF-IDF nearest-neighbour correction---that are switched on only when a leave-one-out test on the acquired labels shows a clear improvement. A streaming acquisition policy removes near-duplicate candidates. Because the test data are private, we reproduce the official protocol locally on (i) a real testbed of \RNPairs{} subject--benchmark pairs generated with four small open models (Qwen2.5 1.5B/3B/7B, Llama~3.2 3B) on GSM8K, SVAMP and BIG-Bench-Hard, and (ii) a synthetic two-parameter IRT testbed with oracle bounds. The shipped predictor reaches a Brier ALC of \RFullAlc{} (95\% CI \RFullAlcLo--\RFullAlcHi) on the real testbed against \REmpAlc{} for the organisers' empirical-mean baseline and 0.25 for the constant predictor, and \SFullAlc{} against \SEmpAlc{} on the synthetic testbed. Oracle analysis splits the remaining headroom into two comparable parts---rate estimation at small budgets, which only a better prior can reduce, and item-level discrimination, which 31 labels cannot deliver: the gated item-level components change the ALC by \SPairFullShrinkMean{} (synthetic) and \RPairFullShrinkMean{} (real) relative to shrinkage alone, sentence embeddings did not beat TF-IDF, and uncertainty sampling did not beat random acquisition. The predictor is pure standard-library Python, deterministic, and runs at about \SmokeCallsPerSec{} calls per second on one CPU core; the submission ZIP \ValidatorStatus{} the organisers' validator. Code and text were produced with the help of an LLM coding assistant (Claude).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Evaluating a new AI system on the full set of available benchmarks is expensive, and the number of benchmarks keeps growing~\citep{liang2022helm}. A line of recent work therefore asks how well performance can be \emph{predicted} rather than measured: from a few hundred carefully chosen items~\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks,perlitz2024efficient}, from the scores of other models~\citep{ruan2024observational}, or from a handful of labelled instances of the new model itself~\citep{pacchiardi2024hundred}. PAIEC~\citep{paiec2026} turns this into a forecasting task with a precise interface: a function \texttt{predict([subject, item], labeled)} must return $\Pr(\text{correct})$ for one subject--item pair, where \texttt{labeled} contains the labels already acquired on the same subject--benchmark pair at budgets $n\in\{0,1,3,7,15,31\}$. Pairs are scored by the Brier score~\citep{brier1950} at each budget and aggregated into a Brier ALC with weights $(0.1,0.2,0.2,0.2,0.2,0.1)$---the trapezoidal area under the Brier-versus-$\log_2(n{+}1)$ curve.

Two facts about this metric shaped our design. First, 60\% of the weight sits at $n\le 7$, where only the pair-level success rate can be estimated with any precision; item-level discrimination can pay off only at $n=15$ and $n=31$, which together carry 30\%. Second, the organisers' empirical-mean baseline is \emph{worse} than the constant 0.5 at $n=1$ (its prediction is 0 or 1), so the single most valuable ingredient is a well-shrunk estimate of the pair rate. Our predictor therefore \emph{shrinks first}: an attribute-based prior at $n=0$, a beta-binomial posterior mean of the pair rate for $n\ge1$~\citep{efron1975}, and only then item-level adaptation, which is \emph{gated} by a leave-one-out (LOO) test on the acquired labels so that it is applied only when the pair's own labels show that it helps.

We also faced a constraint that defines the submission: the official training release (\texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db}) is gated behind a Hugging Face login, and the machine used for this work had no credentials; we deliberately did not create any account. The predictor therefore contains \emph{no parameter fitted on any response data}. Every constant is a documented prior (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and the report tests their sensitivity. We see this as a strong, fully reproducible baseline for the competition and as a measurement of how far test-time adaptation alone can go.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1)~A standard-library-only predictor and streaming acquisition policy that pass the official validator and run at hundreds of calls per second. (2)~A local reproduction of the evaluation protocol---pairs, 50/50 acquisition/evaluation pools, a fixed random stream, nested budgets, Brier ALC and ECE---on a \emph{real} testbed of \RNPairs{} pairs built with local open models and on a synthetic IRT testbed with known item probabilities. (3)~An analysis with paired bootstrap confidence intervals of what helps (prior, shrinkage), what helps marginally (item-level components), and what does not (sentence embeddings over TF-IDF, uncertainty sampling over random acquisition), together with oracle bounds that locate the remaining headroom.

\section{Task, interface and metric}
\label{sec:task}

A \emph{subject} is a dictionary of strings (\texttt{normalized\_name}, \texttt{provider}, \texttt{release\_date}, \texttt{access\_date}, \texttt{harness}, \texttt{harness\_version}, \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, \texttt{subject\_features\_extra}); an \emph{item} has \texttt{item\_content} (the task text), \texttt{item\_features} (e.g.\ \texttt{tier=medium;points=1}), \texttt{interactors}, and an anonymous \texttt{benchmark\_id} shared by all items of a benchmark. Missing values are empty strings. For every subject--benchmark pair with at least 80 distinct items, the evaluator splits the items 50/50 into an acquisition pool and an evaluation pool; acquisition items are never scored. Candidates from the acquisition pool arrive in a fixed random order and an optional \texttt{acquisition\_function(input, prediction, labeled, context)} returns \texttt{True} to reveal a label (31 at most); without it the evaluator samples labels uniformly. The nested label sets after 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 reveals are passed to \texttt{predict} for every evaluation item, giving Brier scores $B_0,\dots,B_{31}$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ALC} = 0.1\,B_0 + 0.2\,B_1 + 0.2\,B_3 + 0.2\,B_7 + 0.2\,B_{15} + 0.1\,B_{31},
\label{eq:alc}
\end{equation}
averaged with equal weight over pairs (lower is better). Expected calibration error (ECE, ten equal-width bins) is reported as a diagnostic~\citep{guo2017calibration}. Test benchmarks are disjoint from the training benchmarks. The evaluation runs offline (no package installation, no model downloads at run time, 8 hours per run).

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{What we could and could not use.} The official training data (\texttt{measurement-db}: item-level responses of many subjects on curated benchmarks) require accepting terms with a Hugging Face account; without credentials we could not download them, and we did not try to obtain them in any other way. We also inspected the organisers' inventory of eligible benchmarks (1,261 entries with slug, authors, code and data URLs and venue, mostly NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, ICLR and ICML papers from 2024--2026) but did not fit anything on any of them. Consequently, \emph{no training data of any kind entered the shipped predictor}. The two local testbeds below were used only to evaluate design choices and to report the numbers in this paper; the handful of constants whose values were influenced by them are identified in Section~\ref{sec:method} and probed by sensitivity analyses in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Real Ollama testbed.}
To obtain item-level correctness labels of real language models we ran four small open-weight models locally with Ollama (Q4\_K\_M quantisation, greedy decoding, CPU only): Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B, 3B and 7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Three public benchmarks were used, none of which appears in the PAIEC inventory (only a derived variant, \texttt{mr\_gsm8k}, does): the first 200 GSM8K test items, the first 200 SVAMP test items (with the SVAMP problem type as \texttt{item\_features}), and a 300-item mix of ten BIG-Bench-Hard subtasks (30 items each, subtask name as \texttt{item\_features}). The 7B model was run on SVAMP only and its run was cut short at 126 items because the shared Ollama server was saturated by other jobs; a pair needs 80 items, so it is kept. Two harnesses are represented: a direct-answer prompt (``reply with only the final answer''; exact match after numeric or option normalisation; the three smaller models on all three benchmarks) and, for GSM8K/SVAMP, an earlier 8-shot chain-of-thought run of the Qwen models whose greedy answers we re-used. Each (model, harness, benchmark) triple with at least 80 items is one pair, giving \RNPairsMeta{} pairs, \RNItemsTotal{} labelled items, and success rates from \RAccMin\% to \RAccMax\% (Table~\ref{tab:testbed} in the appendix). The subject dictionaries carry the real model names, providers, release dates and harness names so that the budget-0 prior is exercised on realistic inputs. Generation took about two hours of wall-clock time on a shared CPU; the raw records (prompt, raw answer, normalised answer, label) are released with the code.

\paragraph{Synthetic IRT testbed.}
Because the real testbed is small (small models, three benchmarks), we complement it with a generator that follows a two-parameter item response model~\citep{lord1980,embretson2000}. Subject $s$ has ability $\theta_s$ correlated with its visible attributes (release year, reasoning effort, parameter count) plus noise; benchmark $b$ has a location $\mu_b$, a discrimination $a_b$ and $K=5$ topic clusters with difficulty offsets $c_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}^2)$; item $i$ in cluster $k(i)$ has difficulty $b_i=\mu_b+c_{k(i)}+\gamma\,z_{\mathrm{len}}(i)+\varepsilon_i$ and $\Pr(y_{si}=1)=\sigma(a_b(\theta_s-b_i))$. Item texts mix cluster-specific vocabulary (35\% of tokens), benchmark template words, ambient vocabulary and numbers, so that lexical similarity carries difficulty information exactly when $\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}>0$; half of the benchmarks expose a \texttt{tier=easy/medium/hard} feature derived from difficulty terciles. The main setting uses \SNSubjects{} subjects $\times$ \SNBenchmarks{} benchmarks $=$ \SNPairs{} pairs with 100--240 items each, $\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSigma$ and $\gamma=\SGamma$; a sweep varies both. Because the true probabilities are known, we can report two oracle references: the Brier score of the true item probabilities (the floor of any predictor) and that of the evaluation-set success rate used as a constant (the floor of any predictor without item-level information).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

All constants below are hand-set and shipped in \texttt{model.py}; the few whose values were adjusted after looking at the local testbeds are marked $^\dagger$ and have sensitivity analyses in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Budget-0 prior.}
A logistic prior $p_0=\sigma(z)$ with $z=\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}+z_{\mathrm{item}},\pm\CPriorCap)$, so $p_0\in[\CPriorPLo,\CPriorPHi]$. The subject part is $z_{\mathrm{subj}}=\CSubjectShrink\cdot(\CYearSlope\,\mathrm{clip}(t-2024.5,\pm2) + e + \CSizeSlope\,\mathrm{clip}(\log_2(N/30\mathrm{B}),\pm4) + \CHarness\,\mathbb 1[\text{non-default harness}])$, where $t$ is the release year (fractional), $e\in\{0,\CEffortLow,\CEffortMid,\CEffortHigh\}$ encodes an empty, low, other or high \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, and $N$ is a parameter count parsed from the name (``7b'', ``70B''); when no count is present, name tokens such as \emph{mini/nano/flash} contribute \CSmallName{} and \emph{pro/opus/ultra} contribute \CLargeName. The item part parses \texttt{key=value} pairs in \texttt{item\_features}: a tier/difficulty/level value matching \emph{hard/expert/\dots} adds \CTierHard, \emph{easy/basic/\dots} adds \CTierEasy, a numeric level $\ell\in[1,5]$ adds $-\CTierNum(\ell-3)$, and points above one subtract \CPoints{} each (capped at five); finally the item length adds $-\CLength\,\mathrm{clip}(\log_2(L/600),\pm3)$ for $L$ characters. The directions encode common knowledge (newer, larger, reasoning-enabled subjects do better; harder, longer, higher-value items are failed more often); the magnitudes were chosen to move the prior by at most a few tenths of a logit per attribute and were not tuned on either testbed.

\paragraph{Pair-level shrinkage.}
Given $n$ acquired labels $y_1,\dots,y_n$ of the same pair with item logits $z_{\mathrm{item}}^{(j)}$ and mean $\bar z$, the prior rate is $r=\sigma(\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}+\bar z))$ and the posterior mean of a Beta--binomial model with prior strength $\alpha_0=\CAlpha^\dagger$ pseudo-counts is $\rho=(\alpha_0 r+\sum_j y_j)/(\alpha_0+n)$~\citep{efron1975}. The pair estimate for a target item is $p_{\mathrm{pair}}=\sigma(\mathrm{logit}\,\rho + z_{\mathrm{item}}-\bar z)$, i.e.\ the item cues shift the pair rate on the logit scale.

\paragraph{Item-level component 1: ridge features.}
From $n\ge\CRidgeMinN$ labels, three cheap features of the item text---log length, digit density and log(1+number of sentences)---are standardised over the acquired items and a logistic regression with offset $\mathrm{logit}\,p_{\mathrm{pair}}$ and ridge penalty $\lambda=\CRidgeLambda$ is fitted by six Newton steps; its adjustment $\mathrm{clip}(\beta^\top \tilde f,\pm\CRidgeCap)$ is added to the target's logit.

\paragraph{Item-level component 2: TF-IDF nearest neighbours.}
Item texts (content, features and interactors, truncated to \CMaxTextChars{} characters) are tokenised into lower-case words and numbers; the acquired set defines the vocabulary and document frequencies, with sublinear term frequency, smoothed idf and $\ell_2$ normalisation (bigram weight \CBigramWeight; target terms absent from the acquired vocabulary are dropped: \CRestrictOov). With cosine similarities $s_j$ between the target and acquired item $j$ and weights $w_j=(\kappa s_j)^2$, the correction is a shrunken kernel-weighted residual,
\begin{equation}
p = \mathrm{clip}\Big(a + \frac{n}{n+N_0}\cdot\frac{\sum_j w_j\,(y_j-a)}{\tau+\sum_j w_j}\Big),\qquad N_0=\CKnnN,\ \tau=\CTau,
\end{equation}
where the anchor $a$ is the pair estimate (optionally including the ridge adjustment). The default scale is $\kappa=\CKappa$. With at most 31 short documents most bigrams are unique, so the bigram terms dominate the vector norms and cosine similarities are small (typically below 0.15 even for lexically related items); this deliberately keeps the correction small unless items share many terms. Section~\ref{sec:results} compares this with a sharper unigram-only representation.

\paragraph{Leave-one-out gating.}
From $n\ge\CLooMinN$ labels, every configuration in $\{\text{ridge off, on}\}\times\{\kappa\in\{0,\CKappas\}\}$ ($\kappa=0$ means no $k$NN) is scored by its leave-one-out Brier score on the acquired labels themselves: label $j$ is predicted from the other $n-1$ labels, including a leave-one-out shrinkage estimate and a leave-one-out ridge fit. The best configuration replaces plain shrinkage only if its mean per-item improvement exceeds \CLooSeMult{} standard error(s) of that mean$^\dagger$; otherwise the predictor returns $p_{\mathrm{pair}}$. This makes adaptation \emph{opt-in per pair and budget}: a pair whose labels show no exploitable structure is predicted by shrinkage alone. Below \CLooMinN{} labels no item-level component is used at all (in preliminary runs an always-on $k$NN correction from one or three labels only added noise at $B_1$ and $B_3$, the budgets that carry most of the metric).

\paragraph{Acquisition policy.}
\texttt{labeling.py} implements the streaming interface and never raises. Candidates are accepted in stream order (which, being uniformly random, yields the same distribution of labels as the evaluator's default) except that a candidate whose maximum TF-IDF cosine similarity to an already acquired item exceeds \CDedupSim{} is skipped while the remaining budget is not urgent (fewer than 90\% of the remaining candidates needed); it always accepts when the remaining candidates are needed to spend the budget and returns \texttt{False} once the budget is exhausted. We compare this \emph{novelty} filter with the evaluator's random policy, with plain stream order, with the organisers' uncertainty band $|p-0.5|\le0.15$~\citep{paiec_baselines_2026}, and with a novelty-plus-uncertainty variant (skip candidates with $|p-0.5|>\CUncSkip$ once \CUncMinN{} labels are known).

\paragraph{Calibration and robustness.}
Predictions are clipped to $[\CClipLo,\CClipHi]$. \texttt{predict} is wrapped in a fallback chain (full model $\to$ shrinkage only $\to$ smoothed label mean $\to$ 0.5), accepts labels given as booleans, numbers or strings, tolerates missing fields and non-dictionary inputs, and is deterministic.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The submission ZIP (\ZipKB{} kB; members \ZipMembers) contains no \texttt{models.txt} and no \texttt{requirements.txt}: \texttt{model.py} imports only \texttt{math} and \texttt{re}, and \texttt{labeling.py} additionally \texttt{importlib}, \texttt{os} and \texttt{sys} to load its sibling \texttt{model.py} by path. It loads no files and makes no network calls, so the evaluator's offline policy is irrelevant to it. Two in-process caches (tokenised texts; statistics of each acquired set, including the LOO selection) only speed up repeated calls and never change a result. The organisers' \texttt{check\_submission\_zip.py} (commit \texttt{cc88a959}) \ValidatorStatus{} on the ZIP; our own smoke test calls \texttt{predict} with the organisers' sample inputs at every budget, with and without \texttt{benchmark\_id}, with malformed labels and inputs (\SmokeNCases{} cases) and checks determinism. Mean latency is \SmokeMsShort{} ms per call on short items and \SmokeMsLong{} ms per call at budget 31 with 300-word items (maximum \SmokeMaxMsLong{} ms when a new acquired set triggers the LOO selection), i.e.\ about \SmokeCallsPerSec{} calls per second or \SmokeCallsPerHour{} million calls per hour on one core of the development laptop---far below the 8-hour limit even for hundreds of thousands of evaluation calls.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Our harness reproduces the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:task}: items of each pair are split 50/50 with a seeded shuffle, the acquisition pool is streamed in a seeded random order, the policy under test selects up to 31 labels, and the nested prefixes of that one trajectory are passed to every predictor for every evaluation item, so all methods are compared on identical labels. The evaluator's default policy is reproduced as accept-with-probability $\text{labels\_remaining}/\text{items\_remaining}$. We report Brier ALC with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over pairs (\NBoot{} resamples), paired bootstrap differences between methods (same pairs, same trajectories), per-budget Brier scores and pooled ECE. Baselines: the constant 0.5; the organisers' empirical mean (their code, which returns 0.5 without matching labels and the raw mean otherwise); our prior alone; shrinkage alone; shrinkage with each item-level component always on; and the shipped LOO-gated model. Everything runs on CPU; the full suite takes about an hour with four worker processes.

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.}

The item-level components are a much smaller effect. Against shrinkage, always-on $k$NN changes the ALC by \SPairKnnShrinkMean{} [\SPairKnnShrinkLo, \SPairKnnShrinkHi] (synthetic) and \RPairKnnShrinkMean{} [\RPairKnnShrinkLo, \RPairKnnShrinkHi] (real); always-on ridge by \SPairRidgeShrinkMean{} [\SPairRidgeShrinkLo, \SPairRidgeShrinkHi] and \RPairRidgeShrinkMean{} [\RPairRidgeShrinkLo, \RPairRidgeShrinkHi]; the LOO-gated full model by \SPairFullShrinkMean{} [\SPairFullShrinkLo, \SPairFullShrinkHi] (\SPairFullShrinkSig) and \RPairFullShrinkMean{} [\RPairFullShrinkLo, \RPairFullShrinkHi] (\RPairFullShrinkSig). At $n=31$ alone the gated model changes the Brier score by \SPairFullShrinkBThirtyOneMean{} [\SPairFullShrinkBThirtyOneLo, \SPairFullShrinkBThirtyOneHi] (synthetic) and \RPairFullShrinkBThirtyOneMean{} [\RPairFullShrinkBThirtyOneLo, \RPairFullShrinkBThirtyOneHi] (real). We report these as what they are: item-level adaptation from at most 31 labels is a marginal gain whose sign has to be protected by the gate.

\paragraph{Headroom.}
The oracle rows explain why. On the synthetic testbed, knowing the true probability of every item gives an ALC of \SOrPAlc, while knowing only the evaluation-set success rate gives \SOrRateAlc: perfect item-level discrimination is worth \SHeadroomItem{} ALC, and it would have to be recovered from 15--31 labels at the two budgets that carry 30\% of the weight. Shrinkage (\SShrinkAlc) is \SHeadroomRate{} above the rate oracle; that gap is the price of estimating a rate from few labels, concentrated at $n\le 3$ ($B_1$: \SHeadroomRateBOne{} above the oracle; $B_{31}$: \SHeadroomRateBThirtyOne), and it can only be reduced by a better prior, i.e.\ by training data. On the real testbed the rate oracle is \ROrRateAlc{} against \RShrinkAlc{} for shrinkage (gap \RHeadroomRate; $B_1$: \RHeadroomRateBOne, $B_{31}$: \RHeadroomRateBThirtyOne). The two parts of the headroom are of similar size, but they call for different remedies: the rate part needs a prior fitted to real subjects and benchmarks; the item part needs item difficulty information from outside the pair.

\paragraph{Prior strength.}
Figure~\ref{fig:sens}(left) and the appendix give the ALC for $\alpha_0\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. The best value differs between testbeds (shrinkage: \SAlphaBestShrink{} on synthetic, \RAlphaBestShrink{} on real), reflecting the wider spread of pair rates in the synthetic generator; the ALC of the full model varies by \SAlphaFullRange{} (synthetic) and \RAlphaFullRange{} (real) over the range, so the choice matters at the third decimal. We ship $\alpha_0=\CAlpha$, the value with the smallest worst-case regret over the two testbeds, and flag it as the constant most worth revisiting with access to real training data: $\alpha_0$ should match the spread of success rates across the pairs actually evaluated.

\paragraph{When does text help?}
The synthetic sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}, right; Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) varies how much difficulty is explained by topic. With no topic or length signal ($\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSweepASigma$, $\gamma=\SSweepAGamma$) the full model's paired difference to shrinkage is \SSweepAPairFullShrinkMean{} [\SSweepAPairFullShrinkLo, \SSweepAPairFullShrinkHi]---the gate's cost when there is nothing to find; at the strongest setting ($\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSweepESigma$) it is \SSweepEPairFullShrinkMean{} [\SSweepEPairFullShrinkLo, \SSweepEPairFullShrinkHi], while the item-level headroom (rate oracle minus true-probability oracle) grows from \SSweepAHeadroomItem{} to \SSweepEHeadroomItem. The sign of the item-level effect does turn with the signal strength, but its size stays two orders of magnitude below the headroom: the gate protects against harm, it cannot create information.

\paragraph{Sentence embeddings versus TF-IDF.}
Replacing the TF-IDF representation by unit-normalised \texttt{\REmbModel} embeddings (computed offline for the real testbed; the model is on the validator's allow-list via \texttt{models.txt}, so it could have been shipped) changes the full model's ALC from \REmbTfidfFullAlc{} to \REmbMinilmFullAlc{} (paired \REmbPairFullMean{} [\REmbPairFullLo, \REmbPairFullHi], \REmbPairFullSig) and the always-on $k$NN variant from \REmbTfidfKnnAlc{} to \REmbMinilmKnnAlc{} (\REmbPairKnnMean{} [\REmbPairKnnLo, \REmbPairKnnHi], \REmbPairKnnSig). With no measurable benefit and a real cost (a neural dependency, model loading and about 10~ms per embedding on CPU), we ship TF-IDF. Appendix~\ref{app:ablations} reports two further ablations: the LOO gate against always-on components and against its margin, and a sharper unigram TF-IDF, which separates topics better but makes the $k$NN worse.

\paragraph{Replication and calibration.}
Regenerating the synthetic testbed with two further seeds gives full-model ALCs of \SSeedOneFullAlc{} and \SSeedTwoFullAlc{} (empirical mean \SSeedOneEmpAlc{} and \SSeedTwoEmpAlc; shrinkage \SSeedOneShrinkAlc{} and \SSeedTwoShrinkAlc). On the real testbed, four further acquisition trajectories give full-model ALCs between \RSeedsFullAlcMin{} and \RSeedsFullAlcMax{} (empirical mean \RSeedsEmpAlcMin--\RSeedsEmpAlcMax), and the full model beats the empirical mean significantly in \RSeedsNFullEmpSig{} of \RSeedsN{} trajectories. A 3$\times$3 grid over the $k$NN scale and anchor ($\kappa\in\{1,2,4\}$, $\tau\in\{1,2,4\}$) spans \SGridBestFullAlc--\SGridWorstFullAlc{} on a smaller synthetic generator, i.e.\ the kernel constants matter little. Pooled ECE (Table~\ref{tab:main}) is \SFullEce{} (synthetic) and \RFullEce{} (real) for the full model against \SEmpEce{} and \REmpEce{} for the empirical mean and \SConstEce/\RConstEce{} for the constant; shrinkage towards a sensible prior is also what fixes calibration at small budgets.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Local-versus-official gap.} Our real testbed covers four small open models on three benchmarks with two harnesses; the official test set contains frontier systems on many held-out benchmarks. In particular the subject part of the prior (release date, reasoning effort, size) has essentially no variance on our testbed and is validated only on the synthetic generator, whose attribute--ability relation we chose ourselves. Absolute ALC values will differ; we expect the \emph{ordering} shrinkage $>$ empirical mean $>$ constant at small budgets to transfer because it follows from the metric, but the sign and size of the item-level effects are testbed-dependent. \emph{No training data.} With \texttt{measurement-db} the prior and $\alpha_0$ could be fitted rather than set by hand; this is the obvious next step and would mostly affect $B_0$ and $B_1$. \emph{Small real testbed.} \RNPairs{} pairs give wide confidence intervals; several differences we report are not significant and are stated as such. \emph{Stateless by design.} The predictor uses only the labels passed to the current call; it does not pool labels across subjects on the same benchmark, which the interface would in principle allow through \texttt{benchmark\_id} but which we considered outside the spirit of the task and fragile under concurrent evaluation. \emph{Weak text signal.} With at most 31 labels, lexical similarity recovers little of the available item-level headroom on either testbed; richer item models would need data beyond the pair. \emph{Untested acquisition filter.} The novelty filter never fired on either testbed, so its effect on benchmarks with templated or repeated items is untested; on our testbeds it is exactly stream order.

\section{Additional ablations}
\label{app:ablations}

\paragraph{Gating.}
Replacing the LOO gate by always-on components at their default settings gives \SLooAlwaysFullAlc{} versus \SLooGatedFullAlc{} (synthetic; paired gated$-$always \SLooPairGatedAlwaysMean{} [\SLooPairGatedAlwaysLo, \SLooPairGatedAlwaysHi], \SLooPairGatedAlwaysSig) and \RLooAlwaysFullAlc{} versus \RLooGatedFullAlc{} (real; \RLooPairGatedAlwaysMean{} [\RLooPairGatedAlwaysLo, \RLooPairGatedAlwaysHi], \RLooPairGatedAlwaysSig). The gate pays where always-on components overfit (the synthetic ridge) and costs a little where a component carries real signal (on the real testbed longer GSM8K problems are failed more often, which the always-on ridge exploits at $n\ge 15$); the second effect is within noise. Requiring 0, 1 or 2 standard errors of LOO improvement gives full-model ALCs of \SLooMultZeroFullAlc, \SLooMultOneFullAlc{} and \SLooMultTwoFullAlc{} (synthetic) and \RLooMultZeroFullAlc, \RLooMultOneFullAlc{} and \RLooMultTwoFullAlc{} (real); we ship the most conservative setting.

\paragraph{Sharper lexical similarity does not help.}
A unigram-only TF-IDF whose target terms are restricted to the acquired vocabulary separates same-topic from other-topic synthetic items far better (mean cosine about 0.3 versus 0.1 in the generator) but makes the always-on $k$NN \emph{worse}: paired against the shipped representation, the always-on $k$NN changes by \STfidfUniKnnVsShippedMean{} [\STfidfUniKnnVsShippedLo, \STfidfUniKnnVsShippedHi] (synthetic) and \RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedMean{} [\RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedLo, \RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedHi] (real), and the gated full model by \STfidfUniFullVsShippedMean{} [\STfidfUniFullVsShippedLo, \STfidfUniFullVsShippedHi] and \RTfidfUniFullVsShippedMean{} [\RTfidfUniFullVsShippedLo, \RTfidfUniFullVsShippedHi]. With a handful of neighbours per topic the kernel-weighted residual is dominated by label noise; the bigram-diluted similarity acts as a regulariser. Item-level signal exists (the oracles show it) but 31 labels are too few to exploit it through similarity alone.

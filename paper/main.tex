\begin{abstract}
We describe our selected submission to the Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC), Codabench submission \SelId. It predicts whether an AI system answers a benchmark item correctly by fitting a random-effects logistic model, once per label-budget checkpoint, to the evaluator's \emph{global} list of acquired labels: benchmark, subject and subject--benchmark effects, plus an exact-item effect learned from other subjects' labels on the same item. It differs from our previous submission (\FmShipId) in three constants of the prior level only: the hand-set subject terms (release year, reasoning effort, model size, harness) are switched off, the prior logit is shifted by \SelIntercept, and the prior standard deviation of the global level is raised from \ShipTauMu{} to \SelTauMu. The change came from the organisers' formative feedback: on the hidden test pool the previous budget-0 prior was worse than predicting 0.5 (Brier \FmShipBZero{} against 0.25). In benchmark-held-out simulations of the official protocol on the training release, the cross-pair model changes the Brier area under the learning curve (ALC) by \XpCADiffSZeroMean{} [\XpCADiffSZeroLo, \XpCADiffSZeroHi] relative to a per-pair predictor, and the new level changes it by \DdSelGainBest{} to \DdSelGainWorstHidden{} in harder, inventory-like populations and by \DdSelCostMin{} to \DdSelCostMax{} in release-like ones. Four formative runs, each a fresh draw of \FmNPairsMin--\FmNPairsMax{} subject--benchmark pairs on the same \FmNBenchAll{} hidden benchmarks, scored \FmShipAlc{} (\FmShipId), \FmNosubjAlc, \FmSelAlc{} (\SelId) and \FmMhundAlc. Every recalibrated run predicted budget 0 better than the constant, but the differences between runs are within draw noise (largest benchmark-matched $|t|=\FmMatchMaxAbsT$). The predictor is standard-library Python. This work was done with an AI coding assistant (Claude).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Evaluating every new AI system on every benchmark is expensive, and the number of benchmarks keeps growing~\citep{liang2022helm}. Recent work therefore predicts performance instead of measuring it: from a few informative items selected with item response theory (IRT)~\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks,kipnis2025metabench,perlitz2024efficient}, adaptively per model~\citep{hofmann2025fluid,zhuang2025position}, from other models' scores~\citep{ruan2024observational,ye2023predictable}, or from a few labelled instances of the new model~\citep{pacchiardi2024hundred}. PAIEC~\citep{paiec2026} makes this a forecasting task with a fixed interface: \texttt{predict([subject, item], labeled)} returns $\Pr(\text{correct})$ for an item of a benchmark from the private test pool, where \texttt{labeled} holds the labels acquired so far, at budgets of $n\in\{0,1,3,7,15,31\}$ labels per subject--benchmark pair.

Our selected submission (\SelId) has two parts. \emph{Cross-pair pooling} (Section~\ref{sec:method}): the evaluator passes one list with the labels of \emph{all} sampled pairs to every prediction, and a random-effects model fitted to that list learns benchmark difficulty from other subjects and item difficulty from other subjects' labels on the same item. \emph{A recalibrated budget-0 level} (Section~\ref{sec:why}): at budget 0 the list is empty and only a prior on visible attributes is available. Our earlier prior encoded ``newer, larger, reasoning-enabled systems do better'' with hand-set coefficients. The formative feedback showed that on the hidden pool this made budget 0 \emph{worse} than the constant 0.5, and the submission switches those terms off and lowers the level.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1)~A cross-pair random-effects predictor for the evaluator's global label list, selected by pre-registered, benchmark-held-out simulation of the official protocol on the training release (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:dev}). (2)~A diagnosis of the budget-0 prior from formative feedback, and a three-constant fix chosen with simulated populations that bracket the hidden pool (Section~\ref{sec:why}). (3)~An honest reading of four formative runs: what they show (budget 0) and what they cannot show (which of the variants is better), with run-to-run noise quantified (Section~\ref{sec:formative}). (4)~Exact rerun instructions and a generated, audited report: every number is produced from result files by a script (Section~\ref{sec:repro}). Earlier rounds and negative results are in the appendices.

\paragraph{Related work.} Item response models, with a subject ability and an item difficulty on a logit scale, are widely used for LLM benchmark data, to rank systems, find informative items and estimate full-benchmark scores from few items~\citep{rodriguez2021evaluation,polo2024tinybenchmarks,kipnis2025metabench}. They fit item parameters offline on hundreds of models. In PAIEC the test benchmarks are new, so difficulty must be learned \emph{during} evaluation from a few labels of a few subjects. We keep the Rasch structure~\citep{lord1980} but replace free parameters by random effects with Gaussian priors, a generalised linear mixed model~\citep{breslow1993}: a benchmark's difficulty is shrunk towards the prior when it has one or three labels and is learned when it has hundreds. Work that predicts a model's scores from other models' scores~\citep{ruan2024observational,ye2023predictable} motivates pooling across subjects; our model does this at the item level, inside one evaluation run.

\section{Task, data and the evaluation interface}
\label{sec:task}

\paragraph{Task and metric.} A \emph{subject} is a dictionary of strings (\texttt{normalized\_name}, \texttt{provider}, \texttt{release\_date}, \texttt{access\_date}, \texttt{harness}, \texttt{harness\_version}, \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, \texttt{subject\_features\_extra}). An \emph{item} has \texttt{item\_content}, \texttt{item\_features}, \texttt{interactors} and an anonymous \texttt{benchmark\_id}. Each subject--benchmark pair with at least 80 distinct items is split 50/50 into an acquisition and an evaluation pool, fixed across submissions. Acquisition candidates arrive in a fixed random order. An optional \texttt{acquisition\_function} returns \texttt{True} to reveal a label (at most 31 per pair); otherwise labels are drawn at random. After 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 labels per pair, every evaluation item is predicted, giving Brier scores~\citep{brier1950} $B_0,\dots,B_{31}$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ALC} = 0.1\,B_0 + 0.2\,B_1 + 0.2\,B_3 + 0.2\,B_7 + 0.2\,B_{15} + 0.1\,B_{31},
\label{eq:alc}
\end{equation}
averaged over pairs (lower is better). Predicting 0.5 gives $B_n=0.25$ on any data.

\paragraph{The global label list.} In the organisers' streaming client (\texttt{tools/streaming\_ingestion.py}, \texttt{run\_streaming}), all predictions of a checkpoint receive the \emph{same} \texttt{labeled} list, and the competition page states that it ``contains [[subject, item], label] entries across sampled subject--benchmark pairs''. At budget $n$ the list holds up to $n$ labels of \emph{every} pair; at budget 0 it is empty. The rules allow this evidence: ``the submitted program may use the attributes and acquired labels supplied through the evaluation interface to adapt during evaluation''. The model uses only anonymous keys (the full attribute dictionary, \texttt{benchmark\_id}, item text) inside one run and keeps nothing across runs.

\paragraph{Data.} \emph{Training release.} The official \texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db} (revision \texttt{\FitRevisionShort}) gives \XpFrNPairs{} pairs of (subject, test condition) on \XpFrNBench{} benchmarks with at least 20 items (\XpFrNPairsEighty{} with 80), \XpFrNRows{} item-level labels (mean over trials) and \XpFrNItems{} distinct items. We use it only to simulate the protocol and to select a few constants; no regression coefficient of the submission is fitted to it. It differs from the hidden population in ways that matter for the prior: \XpFrShareNameBench\% of pairs share (model name, benchmark) with another pair, \DdPopEmptyName\% of eligible pairs have an empty \texttt{normalized\_name}, and the \DdPopNInvBench{} release benchmarks that are rows of the organisers' benchmark inventory are harder than the rest (mean pair success rate \DdPopInvRate{} vs.\ \DdPopNonInvRate). The organisers' curation guidelines restrict competition-eligible base models to 2025--2026 releases~\citep{paiec2026}. \emph{Hidden test pool.} Our four formative runs (Section~\ref{sec:formative}) each scored \FmNPairsMin--\FmNPairsMax{} pairs with \FmNItemsMin--\FmNItemsMax{} evaluation subject--items, always on the same \FmNBenchAll{} benchmark IDs; across runs \FmNSubjAll{} distinct subject IDs appeared and no pair was repeated. \emph{Local testbed.} \XpRealNPairs{} pairs of four small open models run locally on GSM8K, SVAMP and BIG-Bench-Hard (Appendix~\ref{app:olddata}) serve as an engineering check inside the organisers' client.

\paragraph{Budget-0 prior.} The prior logit is $o(s,i)=\mathrm{clip}\big(\lambda\,z_{\mathrm{subj}}(s)+\delta+z_{\mathrm{item}}(i),\pm\CPriorCap\big)$, and at budget 0 the prediction is $\sigma(o)$. The item part $z_{\mathrm{item}}$ reads tier, level and points cues in \texttt{item\_features} and the item length; the hand-set subject part $z_{\mathrm{subj}}$ rewards a recent release date, a high reasoning effort, a large parameter count or size word in the name, and a non-default harness (Appendix~\ref{app:perpair}). The selected submission sets $\lambda=\texttt{SUBJECT\_SHRINK}=\SelSubjectShrink$ and $\delta=\texttt{PRIOR\_INTERCEPT}=\SelIntercept$ (Table~\ref{tab:constants}): every subject starts from the same level, so an item without cues receives $\sigma(\SelIntercept)=\SelPZeroNoCue$, and only item cues move the prior.

\paragraph{Cross-pair random-effects model.} From budget 1 on, for a label $y_e$ of subject $s$ on item $i$ of benchmark $b$ in the global list,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{logit}\,\Pr(y_e=1) = o(s,i) + \mu + v_b + u_s + w_{sb},
\label{eq:xp}
\end{equation}
with independent priors $\mu\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_\mu^2)$, $v_b\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_b^2)$, $u_s\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_s^2)$, $w_{sb}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_{sb}^2)$. Subjects are identified by their complete attribute dictionary and benchmarks by \texttt{benchmark\_id}. The MAP estimate is computed by backfitting: one Newton step per effect type and sweep, $\theta_g \leftarrow \theta_g + \big(\sum_{e\in g}(y_e-p_e)-\theta_g/\tau^2\big)/\big(\sum_{e\in g}p_e(1-p_e)+1/\tau^2\big)$, at most \XpSweeps{} sweeps (penalised quasi-likelihood~\citep{breslow1993}). The target's latent logit $m=o+\hat\mu+\hat v_b+\hat u_s+\hat w_{sb}$ (zero for groups without labels) has approximate posterior variance $V=\sum_g 1/(H_g+\tau_g^{-2})$ ($\tau_g^2$ for an unseen group), and $p=\sigma\big(m/\sqrt{1+\pi V/8}\big)$~\citep{mackay1992}. \emph{Exact-item effect.} If the target item (same \texttt{benchmark\_id} and text) was labelled for other subjects, an item effect $d\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_d^2)$ is estimated from those labels by \XpItemNewton{} Newton steps on the conditional scale (their stage-1 logits multiplied by $c=\sqrt{1+\pi\tau_d^2/8}$), and $p=\sigma\big((c\,m'+\hat d)/\sqrt{1+\pi\,\mathrm{Var}(d)/8}\big)$ with $m'$ the probit-scaled stage-1 logit. Prior standard deviations: $\tau_b=\XpTauBench$, $\tau_s=\XpTauSubj$, $\tau_{sb}=\XpTauPair$, $\tau_d=\XpTauItem$, selected in round 4 (Section~\ref{sec:dev}); $\tau_\mu=\SelTauMu$ in the selected submission (\ShipTauMu{} before). With the prior level lowered, a wide $\tau_\mu$ lets the global level be set by the labels from budget 1 on rather than held near the prior.

\paragraph{Acquisition: fresh first.} A label on an item nobody has labelled can help every subject whose evaluation pool contains that item. \texttt{labeling.py} accepts candidates in stream order (a uniformly random sample, as the default policy) but skips a candidate whose item is already in the global list for another subject while $\text{labels\_remaining}<\text{items\_remaining}$, so the budget is always spent. It takes no \texttt{prediction} argument, so the client does not call \texttt{predict} during acquisition.

\paragraph{Implementation.} \texttt{model.py} and \texttt{labeling.py} use only the Python standard library, need no data files, models or network, and are deterministic. Because \texttt{labeled} is identical for all calls of a checkpoint, the parsed list and the fit are cached at module level under a fingerprint and replaced atomically under a lock. For the selected files, a \CkSelBigEntries-label list is fitted in \CkSelBigFit{} s and a cached call takes \CkSelBigMs{} ms on one laptop core (Table~\ref{tab:xpruntime} for other sizes). Any failure falls back to the per-pair predictor of our earlier rounds (Appendix~\ref{app:perpair}), then to the prior, then to 0.5.

\section{Why recalibrate the budget-0 level}
\label{sec:why}

\paragraph{What the formative feedback showed.} Codabench returns, per run, the Brier ALC, the ECE and the per-budget scores of every pair (anonymous IDs). We use only aggregates over pairs. For our previous submission \FmShipId, budget 0 scored $B_0=\FmShipBZero$, \emph{above} the constant's 0.25. Since 0.25 is the constant's score on any draw, this is a comparison within one draw. The budget-0 ECE was \FmShipEceBZero, above the \DdEceBZeroShipMin--\DdEceBZeroShipMax{} that the same predictor shows in all four simulated populations of Appendix~\ref{app:formative}. From $n=1$ on, the cross-pair model learns the level from the list, and the later budgets were in the range of the simulated inventory population ($B_1=\FmShipBOne$ and $B_3=\FmShipBThree$ against \DdInvShipBOne{} and \DdInvShipBThree). Two facts about the training release point the same way. On the \DdPopNInvBench{} inventory benchmarks, the hand-set prior is already worse than the constant at budget 0 ($B_0\approx\DdPopInvBZero$; \DdPopInvYBZero{} for subjects released in 2025 or later). And the release-year term raises the prior of every eligible test subject, since all are 2025--2026 releases, which is the wrong direction if the hidden benchmarks are harder than the release. A table of subject ability by model name, learned from the release, does not transfer to held-out benchmarks either (correlation \DdAbilityCorrMin{} to \DdAbilityCorrMax, $R^2\le\DdAbilityRsqMax$; Appendix~\ref{app:deepdive}). So the subject terms are removed rather than re-fitted.

\paragraph{Simulated populations.} We do not know the hidden pool's composition, so we compare the variants in seven populations built from the training release with the round-4 simulator (Section~\ref{sec:dev}; formative-sized test sets of up to ten pairs on one to three benchmarks, benchmark-held-out). They range from release-like (A, and Y25 with only 2025+ subjects) to inventory-like (INV, INV25) and harder (MID, HARD), plus SHIFT, in which every prior offset is one logit too high (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}). The level matters almost only at budget 0: from $n=1$ on, the global and benchmark effects take the level from the labels, so no later budget changes by more than \DdSelMaxDeltaLater{} in any population.

\paragraph{The choice.} Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}. Switching the subject terms off alone (\FmNosubjId{}) changes the ALC by only \DdNosubjGainWorst{} to \DdNosubjGainBest{} outside release-like data. Adding the \SelIntercept{} shift (\SelId{}) changes it by \DdMidSelMean{} [\DdMidSelLo, \DdMidSelHi] on MID, \DdHardSelMean{} [\DdHardSelLo, \DdHardSelHi] on HARD and \DdShiftSelMean{} on SHIFT; it is not significantly different on INV (\DdInvSelMean) and INV25 (\DdInvYSelMean) and costs \DdASelMean{} [\DdASelLo, \DdASelHi] on A and \DdYSelMean{} on Y25. A shift of \MhundIntercept{} (\FmMhundId{}) changes the ALC relative to \SelId{} by \DdMhundMinusSelBest{} to \DdMhundMinusSelWorst{} in those five populations but more than doubles the release-like cost (\DdAMhundMean{} on A, \DdYMhundMean{} on Y25). Because the feedback indicates that, at budget 0, the hidden pool does not behave like the release, we chose \SelId: significant gains in \DdSelNSigGain{} of the five non-release-like populations, no significant loss in any of them, and a bounded cost if we are wrong. The choice was recorded in our deep-dive notes (\texttt{DEEP\_DIVE.md}) while the candidates' formative runs were pending, together with a rule for revising it: switch only if the selected run looked release-like at budget 0 \emph{and} its budget-0 Brier, matched by benchmark, were worse than the previous submission's. It was \FmSelRuleBZero{} (\FmMatchSelShipBZeroMean, se \FmMatchSelShipBZeroSe), so the choice stood.

The final ranking uses one common test subset for all teams, where only the expected score matters.

Our selected run (\FmSelAlc) would sit at about place \LbRankSel, our previous one (\FmShipAlc) at \LbRankShip. \subsection{Development evidence for the cross-pair model}
\label{sec:dev}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The round-4 simulator rebuilds the official protocol on the training release with five benchmark-held-out folds. A \emph{test set} is a set of pairs of one fold sharing one global list. Per pair, items are split 50/50 and the acquisition pool is permuted; at budget $n$ every prediction of the test set receives the union of all its pairs' first $n$ labels. The primary configuration A draws 1--3 random benchmarks and 10 pairs with 100 items each ($\le$1,000 subject--item pairs, as in formative runs), 300 test sets per fold. Configurations B--E vary the composition (Appendix~\ref{app:round4}). The design, the grid of \XpGridN{} prior settings, nested selection and a ship rule were pre-registered (\texttt{experiments/results/round4/PREREG.md}, SHA-256 prefix \texttt{\XpPreregSha}) before any outcome was computed. 95\% CIs come from a cluster bootstrap over benchmarks (2,000 resamples).

\paragraph{Cross-pair gain.} Table~\ref{tab:xpconfigs}. In configuration A the cross-pair model reaches \XpCAXpAlc{} against \XpCASZeroAlc{} for the per-pair predictor (\XpCADiffSZeroMean{} [\XpCADiffSZeroLo, \XpCADiffSZeroHi], lower on \XpCADiffSZeroFracNeg\% of units), closing \XpCAGapClosed\% of the gap to the pair-rate oracle. The gain holds when cross-pair evidence only comes from different models (E: \XpCEDiffSZeroMean{} [\XpCEDiffSZeroLo, \XpCEDiffSZeroHi]) and grows when more pairs share the list (B: \XpCBDiffSZeroMean; C: \XpCCDiffSZeroMean). The fresh-first rule changes the ALC by \XpAcqMean{} [\XpAcqLo, \XpAcqHi], almost all at $n\ge15$.

\paragraph{Inside the organisers' client.} Driven by a mock coordinator on the local testbed, the organisers' \texttt{run\_streaming} runs the selected files in \texttt{\CkSelLoopMode} acquisition mode with \CkSelLoopAcqPredCalls{} \texttt{predict} calls during acquisition and \CkSelLoopLabels{} labels on each of the \CkSelLoopNPairs{} pairs. \section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{The choice rests on simulation and one budget-0 observation.} The hidden pool's composition is unknown. The seven populations bracket it, but they are all built from the training release, whose subjects (many test-condition variants of one model, many without names or release dates) differ from the 2025--2026 competition subjects. If the hidden pool were release-like, \SelId{} would cost about \DdSelCostMin--\DdSelCostMax{} ALC. \emph{Formative noise.} Each formative run is one draw of \FmNPairsMin--\FmNPairsMax{} pairs; four runs cannot rank the variants (Section~\ref{sec:formative}). We did not estimate how often the previous prior would exceed 0.25 at budget 0 on a release-like draw. Choosing the level after seeing formative feedback is a mild form of tuning on the test pool; we changed three constants once, on the strength of one aggregate and the simulations. \emph{The level is global.} One intercept cannot fix benchmark-specific difficulty at budget 0. In simulation, knowing each test benchmark's mean difficulty would change the ALC by \DdOrcABenchMean{} (release-like) to \DdOrcHardBenchMean{} (hard), and knowing item difficulty by \DdOrcAItemMean{} to \DdOrcHardItemMean{} (Appendix~\ref{app:deepdive}). Nothing in the submission estimates either before labels arrive. \emph{Cross-pair assumptions.} The gain depends on how many pairs share a list; item identity is exact text match within a benchmark; pairs with fewer than 80 items are handled under one assumed rule (Appendix~\ref{app:round4}). \emph{Local testbed.} \XpRealNPairs{} pairs of small models on three benchmarks give wide CIs; it checks engineering, not accuracy on frontier systems.

\section{Per-pair predictor (rounds 1--3; now the fallback and the budget-0 prior)}
\label{app:perpair}


All constants below are hand-set and are still in \texttt{model.py}: the budget-0 prior is the offset of the cross-pair model, the rest is its fallback. Values are those of the round-4 file (\FmShipId); the selected submission (\SelId) differs only in \texttt{SUBJECT\_SHRINK} (\SelSubjectShrink{} instead of \ShipSubjectShrink, which removes the whole subject part below) and \texttt{PRIOR\_INTERCEPT} (\SelIntercept{} instead of \ShipIntercept), besides $\tau_\mu$ of the cross-pair model (Table~\ref{tab:constants}). The few whose values were adjusted after looking at the local testbeds are marked $^\dagger$ and have sensitivity analyses in Appendix~\ref{app:oldresults}.

\paragraph{Budget-0 prior.}
A logistic prior $p_0=\sigma(z)$ with $z=\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}+z_{\mathrm{item}},\pm\CPriorCap)$, so $p_0\in[\CPriorPLo,\CPriorPHi]$. The subject part is $z_{\mathrm{subj}}=\CSubjectShrink\cdot(\CYearSlope\,\mathrm{clip}(t-2024.5,\pm2) + e + \CSizeSlope\,\mathrm{clip}(\log_2(N/30\mathrm{B}),\pm4) + \CHarness\,\mathbb 1[\text{non-default harness}])$, where $t$ is the release year (fractional), $e\in\{0,\CEffortLow,\CEffortMid,\CEffortHigh\}$ encodes an empty, low, other or high \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, and $N$ is a parameter count parsed from the name (``7b'', ``70B''); when no count is present, name tokens such as \emph{mini/nano/flash} contribute \CSmallName{} and \emph{pro/opus/ultra} contribute \CLargeName. A per-provider table (\CNProvider{} entries, inside the multiplier) and an intercept $\delta$ added after it (\texttt{PRIOR\_INTERCEPT}: \ShipIntercept{} in round 4, \SelIntercept{} in the selected submission) complete the subject part; the provider table is empty and is the slot a fitted coefficient table would fill. The item part parses \texttt{key=value} pairs in \texttt{item\_features}: a tier/difficulty/level value matching \emph{hard/expert/\dots} adds \CTierHard, \emph{easy/basic/\dots} adds \CTierEasy, a numeric level $\ell\in[1,5]$ adds $-\CTierNum(\ell-3)$, and points above one subtract \CPoints{} each (capped at five); finally the item length adds $-\CLength\,\mathrm{clip}(\log_2(L/600),\pm3)$ for $L$ characters. The directions encode common knowledge (newer, larger, reasoning-enabled subjects do better; harder, longer, higher-value items are failed more often); the magnitudes were chosen to move the prior by at most a few tenths of a logit per attribute and were not tuned on either testbed.

\paragraph{Pair-level shrinkage.}
Given $n$ acquired labels $y_1,\dots,y_n$ of the same pair with item logits $z_{\mathrm{item}}^{(j)}$ and mean $\bar z$, the prior rate is $r=\sigma(\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}+\bar z))$ and the posterior mean of a Beta--binomial model with prior strength $\alpha_0=\CAlpha^\dagger$ pseudo-counts is $\rho=(\alpha_0 r+\sum_j y_j)/(\alpha_0+n)$~\citep{efron1975}. The pair estimate for a target item is $p_{\mathrm{pair}}=\sigma(\mathrm{logit}\,\rho + z_{\mathrm{item}}-\bar z)$, i.e.\ the item cues shift the pair rate on the logit scale.

\paragraph{Item-level component 1: ridge features.}
From $n\ge\CRidgeMinN$ labels, three cheap features of the item text---log length, digit density and log(1+number of sentences)---are standardised over the acquired items and a logistic regression with offset $\mathrm{logit}\,p_{\mathrm{pair}}$ and ridge penalty $\lambda=\CRidgeLambda$ is fitted by six Newton steps; its adjustment $\mathrm{clip}(\beta^\top \tilde f,\pm\CRidgeCap)$ is added to the target's logit.

\paragraph{Item-level component 2: TF-IDF nearest neighbours.}
Item texts (content, features and interactors, truncated to \CMaxTextChars{} characters) are tokenised into lower-case words and numbers; the acquired set defines the vocabulary and document frequencies, with sublinear term frequency, smoothed idf and $\ell_2$ normalisation (bigram weight \CBigramWeight; target terms absent from the acquired vocabulary are dropped: \CRestrictOov). With cosine similarities $s_j$ between the target and acquired item $j$ and weights $w_j=(\kappa s_j)^2$, the correction is a shrunken kernel-weighted residual,
\begin{equation}
p = \mathrm{clip}\Big(a + \frac{n}{n+N_0}\cdot\frac{\sum_j w_j\,(y_j-a)}{\tau+\sum_j w_j}\Big),\qquad N_0=\CKnnN,\ \tau=\CTau,
\end{equation}
where the anchor $a$ is the pair estimate (optionally including the ridge adjustment) and $\kappa=\CKappa$ by default. With at most 31 short documents most bigrams are unique, so cosine similarities are small (typically below 0.15 even for related items), which deliberately keeps the correction small unless items share many terms; Appendix~\ref{app:ablations} compares a sharper unigram-only representation.

\paragraph{Leave-one-out gating.}
From $n\ge\CLooMinN$ labels, every configuration in $\{\text{ridge off, on}\}\times\{\kappa\in\{0,\CKappas\}\}$ ($\kappa=0$ means no $k$NN) is scored by its leave-one-out Brier score on the acquired labels themselves: label $j$ is predicted from the other $n-1$ labels, including a leave-one-out shrinkage estimate and a leave-one-out ridge fit. The best configuration replaces plain shrinkage only if its mean per-item improvement exceeds \CLooSeMult{} standard error(s) of that mean$^\dagger$; otherwise the predictor returns $p_{\mathrm{pair}}$. This makes adaptation \emph{opt-in per pair and budget}: a pair whose labels show no exploitable structure is predicted by shrinkage alone. Below \CLooMinN{} labels no item-level component is used at all (in preliminary runs an always-on $k$NN from one or three labels only added noise at $B_1$ and $B_3$).

\paragraph{Acquisition policy.}
\texttt{labeling.py} implements the streaming interface and never raises. Candidates are accepted in stream order (uniformly random, hence the same label distribution as the evaluator's default) except that a candidate whose maximum TF-IDF cosine similarity to an already acquired item exceeds \CDedupSim{} is skipped while fewer than 90\% of the remaining candidates are needed; it always accepts when the remaining candidates are needed to spend the budget. We compare this \emph{novelty} filter with the evaluator's random policy, plain stream order, the organisers' uncertainty band $|p-0.5|\le0.15$~\citep{paiec_baselines_2026}, and a novelty-plus-uncertainty variant (skip $|p-0.5|>\CUncSkip$ once \CUncMinN{} labels are known).


\paragraph{Implementation (round-4 and selected files).} Predictions are clipped to $[\CClipLo,\CClipHi]$; \texttt{predict} is wrapped in a fallback chain (cross-pair model $\to$ per-pair full model $\to$ shrinkage only $\to$ smoothed label mean $\to$ 0.5), accepts labels given as booleans, numbers or strings, tolerates missing fields, malformed entries and non-dictionary inputs, and is deterministic. The round-4 ZIP (\FmShipId; \ZipKB{} kB; members \ZipMembers) contains no \texttt{models.txt} and no \texttt{requirements.txt}: both files import only standard-library modules (\texttt{model.py}: \texttt{array}, \texttt{base64}, \texttt{hashlib}, \texttt{json}, \texttt{math}, \texttt{os}, \texttt{re}, \texttt{sys}, \texttt{threading}, \texttt{zlib}; the round-3 item-text-prior hooks stay inactive because no weight file is shipped; \texttt{labeling.py} loads its sibling \texttt{model.py} by path) and make no network calls. The organisers' \texttt{check\_submission\_zip.py} (commit \texttt{cc88a959}) \ValidatorStatus{} on the ZIP. Our smoke test calls \texttt{predict} with the organisers' sample inputs at every budget and with malformed labels and inputs (\SmokeNCases{} cases), plus \XpSmokeXpCases{} cross-pair cases, and checks determinism; with the cross-pair model enabled the mean latency is \SmokeMsShort{} ms per call on short items and \SmokeMsLong{} ms at budget 31 with 300-word items (maximum \SmokeMaxMsLong{} ms), about \SmokeCallsPerSec{} calls per second on one laptop core.

\section{Rounds 1--3: data, testbeds and the training release}
\label{app:olddata}


\paragraph{What we could and could not use.} The official training data (\texttt{measurement-db}: item-level responses of many subjects on curated benchmarks) require accepting terms with a Hugging Face account. For most of the project we had no credentials and did not try to obtain the data in any other way; the per-pair predictor and all its constants were fixed before any of it was seen. Its public file listing (2026-10-22, revision \texttt{\MdbRevisionShort}) held \MdbNDirs{} benchmark directories: \MdbCoreGb{} GB of \texttt{benchmarks}, \texttt{subjects}, \texttt{items} and \texttt{responses} tables plus \MdbTracesGb{} GB of traces and \MdbAssetsGb{} GB of assets. On that day the author's login became available and we downloaded the \FitNEligible{} competition-eligible directories (binary, item-level responses; the eligibility flags live inside the \texttt{benchmarks} tables) without traces or assets, and used them as described next. We also inspected the organisers' inventory of eligible benchmarks (1,261 entries) but fitted nothing on any of them. The two local testbeds below only evaluate design choices; the few constants they influenced are marked in Appendix~\ref{app:perpair} and probed in Appendix~\ref{app:oldresults}.

\paragraph{The training release: validation and a fit that was not shipped.} \texttt{fit\_prior\_from\_measurement\_db.py} builds one row per (subject, test condition, item) with the mean response over trials as label---\FitNPairs{} pairs with at least 20 distinct items from \FitNBench{} benchmarks, \FitNRows{} rows (pairs capped at \FitMaxItemsPerPair{} items; \FitRelNPairsAlc{} pairs have the 80 items the protocol requires)---and does two things (Appendix~\ref{app:fit}). First, it evaluates the rounds 1--3 prior-plus-shrinkage rule with the protocol in closed form (50/50 split, 31 random acquisitions, Eq.~\ref{eq:alc}; without the item-level components, which change the ALC by $<0.001$ locally): ALC \FitRelHandAlc{} [\FitRelHandAlcLo, \FitRelHandAlcHi] against \FitRelEmpAlc{} [\FitRelEmpAlcLo, \FitRelEmpAlcHi] for the organisers' empirical mean and \FitRelConstAlc{} for the constant, with $\alpha_0=4$ the best of $\{2,4,8\}$. The ordering and the size of the gain measured locally thus transfer to real systems on real benchmarks. Second, it fits an $L_2$-penalised logistic regression on exactly the feature terms of the hand-set prior plus a provider table and benchmark fixed effects (nuisance parameters; the shipped intercept is their mean), pairs weighted equally, with five-fold cross-validation over whole benchmarks. The fitted prior \emph{loses} to the hand-set one on held-out benchmarks at every penalty (budget-0 Brier \FitCMinBZero{}--\FitCMaxBZero{} against \FitRelHandBZero; paired differences at the selected setting \FitPairBZeroMean{} [\FitPairBZeroLo, \FitPairBZeroHi] at budget 0 and \FitPairAlcMean{} [\FitPairAlcLo, \FitPairAlcHi] in ALC), and an intercept-only prior is worse still (\FitRelIntOnlyBZero). The ship-only-if-better rule therefore kept the hand-set constants; in rounds 1--3 no coefficient of the submission derived from the release (round 4 selects the cross-pair model's prior standard deviations on it, Section~\ref{sec:dev}). Where the data speak, the fitted signs agree with the hand-set values; what held-out benchmarks punish is the provider table (Appendix~\ref{app:fit}).

\paragraph{An item-text difficulty prior (pre-registered; not shipped).} Benchmark difficulty dominates the pair rate (benchmark means have standard deviation \FitBenchRateSd) and the attribute prior cannot see it. We therefore tested, with design and ship rule fixed in \texttt{PREREG.md} (SHA-256 \texttt{\TpPreregSha}\dots) before any fit, whether the item text predicts difficulty on \emph{unseen} benchmarks: a ridge regression on \TpBits{} signed-hashed buckets (words, bigrams, character trigrams, \texttt{item\_features}) and 16 text statistics (length, \LaTeX, code, answer choices, \dots), trained on the item logit the hand-set prior leaves unexplained (\TpNTargetItems{} items, benchmarks weighted equally) and stacked onto the prior as $T=Ws+B$ with $W,B$ fitted on out-of-fold scores; shrinkage is unchanged. On the same benchmark-held-out folds, every choice nested inside the training benchmarks, the ALC changes by \TpDiffAlcMean{} [\TpDiffAlcLo, \TpDiffAlcHi] (cluster bootstrap over \TpNClusters{} benchmarks), closing \TpGapZero\%, \TpGapOne\% and \TpGapThree\% of the gap to the pair-rate oracle at $n=0,1,3$. The inner CV chose the largest penalty of the grid in \TpNFoldsAlphaMax{} of \TpNFolds{} folds and the out-of-fold slope $W$ was \emph{negative} in all \TpNFoldsNegW{} (\TpFoldWMin{} to \TpFoldWMax): text patterns learned on some benchmarks point the wrong way on others. Exploratory variants registered afterwards (penalties up to $10^4$; statistics alone) give \TpEOneDiffMean{} and \TpETwoDiffMean, the local testbeds \TpRPairFullMean{} (real, \TpRPairFullSig) and \TpSPairFullMean{} (synthetic, \TpSPairFullSig). Headroom exists---knowing each held-out benchmark's mean rate would gain \TpDiffBenchOracleMean{} ALC---but item text in this form does not reach it; the rule fails and the submission is unchanged (Appendix~\ref{app:textprior}).

\paragraph{Real Ollama testbed.}
To obtain item-level correctness labels of real language models we ran four small open-weight models locally with Ollama (Q4\_K\_M quantisation, greedy decoding, CPU only): Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B, 3B and 7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Three public benchmarks were used, none of which appears in the PAIEC inventory (only a derived variant, \texttt{mr\_gsm8k}, does): the first 200 GSM8K test items, the first 200 SVAMP test items (with the SVAMP problem type as \texttt{item\_features}), and a 300-item mix of ten BIG-Bench-Hard subtasks (30 items each, subtask name as \texttt{item\_features}). Two harnesses are represented: a direct-answer prompt (``reply with only the final answer''; exact match after numeric or option normalisation) and, for GSM8K/SVAMP, an earlier 8-shot chain-of-thought run of the Qwen models whose greedy answers we re-used. Each (model, harness, benchmark) triple with at least 80 items is one pair, giving \RNPairsMeta{} pairs, \RNItemsTotal{} labelled items, and success rates from \RAccMin\% to \RAccMax\% (Table~\ref{tab:testbed} in the appendix). The subject dictionaries carry the real model names, providers, release dates and harness names, so the budget-0 prior sees realistic inputs; the raw records are released with the code.

\paragraph{Synthetic IRT testbed.}
Because the real testbed is small (small models, three benchmarks), we complement it with a generator that follows a two-parameter item response model~\citep{lord1980,embretson2000}. Subject $s$ has ability $\theta_s$ correlated with its visible attributes (release year, reasoning effort, parameter count) plus noise; benchmark $b$ has a location $\mu_b$, a discrimination $a_b$ and $K=5$ topic clusters with difficulty offsets $c_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}^2)$; item $i$ in cluster $k(i)$ has difficulty $b_i=\mu_b+c_{k(i)}+\gamma\,z_{\mathrm{len}}(i)+\varepsilon_i$ and $\Pr(y_{si}=1)=\sigma(a_b(\theta_s-b_i))$. Item texts mix cluster-specific vocabulary (35\% of tokens), benchmark template words, ambient vocabulary and numbers, so that lexical similarity carries difficulty information exactly when $\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}>0$; half of the benchmarks expose a \texttt{tier=easy/medium/hard} feature derived from difficulty terciles. The main setting uses \SNSubjects{} subjects $\times$ \SNBenchmarks{} benchmarks $=$ \SNPairs{} pairs with 100--240 items each, $\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSigma$ and $\gamma=\SGamma$; a sweep varies both. Because the true probabilities are known, we can report two oracle references: the Brier score of the true item probabilities (the floor of any predictor) and that of the evaluation-set success rate used as a constant (the floor of any predictor without item-level information).


\paragraph{Experimental setup.} Our rounds 1--3 harness reproduces the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:task} with \emph{per-pair} label lists (round 4 replaces this by the global list, Section~\ref{sec:dev}): items of each pair are split 50/50 with a seeded shuffle, the acquisition pool is streamed in a seeded random order, the policy under test selects up to 31 labels, and the nested prefixes of that one trajectory are passed to every predictor for every evaluation item, so all methods are compared on identical labels. The evaluator's default policy is reproduced as accept-with-probability $\text{labels\_remaining}/\text{items\_remaining}$. We report Brier ALC with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over pairs (\NBoot{} resamples), paired bootstrap differences between methods (same pairs, same trajectories), per-budget Brier scores and pooled ECE. Baselines: the constant 0.5; the organisers' empirical mean (their code: 0.5 without matching labels, the raw mean otherwise); our prior alone; shrinkage alone; shrinkage with each item-level component always on; the LOO-gated model (shipped in rounds 1--3).

\paragraph{Headroom.}
The oracle rows explain why. On the synthetic testbed, knowing the true probability of every item gives an ALC of \SOrPAlc, knowing only the evaluation-set success rate \SOrRateAlc: perfect item-level discrimination is worth \SHeadroomItem{} ALC, to be recovered from 15--31 labels at budgets that carry 30\% of the weight. Shrinkage (\SShrinkAlc) is \SHeadroomRate{} above the rate oracle; that gap is the price of estimating a rate from few labels, concentrated at $n\le 3$ ($B_1$: \SHeadroomRateBOne; $B_{31}$: \SHeadroomRateBThirtyOne), and only a better prior, i.e.\ training data, can reduce it. On the real testbed the gap is \RHeadroomRate{} (\RShrinkAlc{} vs.\ \ROrRateAlc). The two parts of the headroom are of similar size but call for different remedies: a prior fitted to real subjects and benchmarks, and item difficulty information from outside the pair.

\paragraph{Prior strength.}
Table~\ref{tab:alpha} gives the ALC for $\alpha_0\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. The best value differs between testbeds (shrinkage: \SAlphaBestShrink{} on synthetic, \RAlphaBestShrink{} on real), reflecting the wider spread of pair rates in the synthetic generator; the ALC of the full model varies by \SAlphaFullRange{} (synthetic) and \RAlphaFullRange{} (real) over the range, so the choice matters at the third decimal. On the training release $\alpha_0=4$ gives \FitRelHandAlphaFourAlc{} against \FitRelHandAlphaTwoAlc{} (2) and \FitRelHandAlphaEightAlc{} (8).

\paragraph{When does text help?}
The synthetic sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}, right; Table~\ref{tab:sweep}) varies how much difficulty is explained by topic. With no topic or length signal ($\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSweepASigma$, $\gamma=\SSweepAGamma$) the full model's paired difference to shrinkage is \SSweepAPairFullShrinkMean{} [\SSweepAPairFullShrinkLo, \SSweepAPairFullShrinkHi], the gate's cost when there is nothing to find; at the strongest setting ($\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSweepESigma$) it is \SSweepEPairFullShrinkMean{} [\SSweepEPairFullShrinkLo, \SSweepEPairFullShrinkHi], while the item-level headroom (rate oracle minus true-probability oracle) grows from \SSweepAHeadroomItem{} to \SSweepEHeadroomItem.

\paragraph{Gating.}
Replacing the LOO gate by always-on components at their default settings gives \SLooAlwaysFullAlc{} versus \SLooGatedFullAlc{} (synthetic; paired gated$-$always \SLooPairGatedAlwaysMean{} [\SLooPairGatedAlwaysLo, \SLooPairGatedAlwaysHi], \SLooPairGatedAlwaysSig) and \RLooAlwaysFullAlc{} versus \RLooGatedFullAlc{} (real; \RLooPairGatedAlwaysMean{} [\RLooPairGatedAlwaysLo, \RLooPairGatedAlwaysHi], \RLooPairGatedAlwaysSig). The gate pays where always-on components overfit (the synthetic ridge) and costs a little where a component carries real signal (on the real testbed longer GSM8K problems are failed more often, which the always-on ridge exploits at $n\ge 15$); the second effect is within noise. Requiring 0, 1 or 2 standard errors of LOO improvement gives full-model ALCs of \SLooMultZeroFullAlc, \SLooMultOneFullAlc{} and \SLooMultTwoFullAlc{} (synthetic) and \RLooMultZeroFullAlc, \RLooMultOneFullAlc{} and \RLooMultTwoFullAlc{} (real); we ship the most conservative setting.

\paragraph{Sentence embeddings.}
The embeddings were computed offline for the real testbed (the model is on the validator's allow-list via \texttt{models.txt}, so it could have been shipped). They change the full model's ALC from \REmbTfidfFullAlc{} to \REmbMinilmFullAlc{} (paired \REmbPairFullMean{} [\REmbPairFullLo, \REmbPairFullHi], \REmbPairFullSig) and the always-on $k$NN variant from \REmbTfidfKnnAlc{} to \REmbMinilmKnnAlc{} (\REmbPairKnnMean{} [\REmbPairKnnLo, \REmbPairKnnHi], \REmbPairKnnSig), at the cost of a neural dependency, model loading and about 10~ms per embedding on CPU.

\paragraph{Sharper lexical similarity does not help.}
A unigram-only TF-IDF whose target terms are restricted to the acquired vocabulary separates same-topic from other-topic synthetic items far better (mean cosine about 0.3 versus 0.1 in the generator) but makes the always-on $k$NN \emph{worse}: paired against the shipped representation, the always-on $k$NN changes by \STfidfUniKnnVsShippedMean{} [\STfidfUniKnnVsShippedLo, \STfidfUniKnnVsShippedHi] (synthetic) and \RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedMean{} [\RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedLo, \RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedHi] (real), and the gated full model by \STfidfUniFullVsShippedMean{} [\STfidfUniFullVsShippedLo, \STfidfUniFullVsShippedHi] and \RTfidfUniFullVsShippedMean{} [\RTfidfUniFullVsShippedLo, \RTfidfUniFullVsShippedHi]. With a handful of neighbours per topic the kernel-weighted residual is dominated by label noise; the bigram-diluted similarity acts as a regulariser. Item-level signal exists (the oracles show it) but 31 labels are too few to exploit it through similarity alone.

Plain stream order differs from the evaluator's random policy by \SPolFirstVsRandomMean{} [\SPolFirstVsRandomLo, \SPolFirstVsRandomHi] (synthetic, \SPolFirstVsRandomSig) and \RPolFirstVsRandomMean{} [\RPolFirstVsRandomLo, \RPolFirstVsRandomHi] (real, \RPolFirstVsRandomSig)---both are uniform samples, so this is the trajectory-to-trajectory noise floor of the comparison. The shipped novelty filter gives \SPolNoveltyVsRandomMean{} [\SPolNoveltyVsRandomLo, \SPolNoveltyVsRandomHi] and \RPolNoveltyVsRandomMean{} [\RPolNoveltyVsRandomLo, \RPolNoveltyVsRandomHi]. It does fire on the real testbed: on \RNovPairsFired{} of \RNovNPairs{} pairs (\RNovSkipped{} candidates skipped in total, at most \RNovMaxSkipped{} per pair; templated BIG-Bench-Hard items such as \emph{navigate} and \emph{boolean expressions}, and SVAMP word problems sharing a template, reach cosine \RNovMaxSim), and on none of the \SNovNPairs{} synthetic pairs, whose pseudo-word items never get that similar---yet its ALC equals that of plain stream order to four decimals (\RPolNoveltyFullAlc{} vs.\ \RPolFirstFullAlc): skipping a near-duplicate changes which uniformly random items are labelled, not their distribution. The organisers' uncertainty band gives \SPolBleVsRandomMean{} [\SPolBleVsRandomLo, \SPolBleVsRandomHi] and \RPolBleVsRandomMean{} [\RPolBleVsRandomLo, \RPolBleVsRandomHi], and the novelty-plus-uncertainty variant \SPolNoveltyUncVsRandomMean{} and \RPolNoveltyUncVsRandomMean: selecting by $|p-0.5|$ reorders a near-random sample at small budgets and, once item-level corrections exist, biases it towards items of intermediate predicted difficulty, distorting the pair-rate estimate that carries most of the metric. None of the policies is significantly different from random on either testbed; we ship the one that is closest to random while removing redundant items.

\section{Replication}
\label{app:replication}
Regenerating the synthetic testbed with two further seeds gives full-model ALCs of \SSeedOneFullAlc{} and \SSeedTwoFullAlc{} (empirical mean \SSeedOneEmpAlc{} and \SSeedTwoEmpAlc; shrinkage \SSeedOneShrinkAlc{} and \SSeedTwoShrinkAlc). On the real testbed, four further acquisition trajectories give full-model ALCs between \RSeedsFullAlcMin{} and \RSeedsFullAlcMax{} (empirical mean \RSeedsEmpAlcMin--\RSeedsEmpAlcMax), and the full model beats the empirical mean significantly in \RSeedsNFullEmpSig{} of \RSeedsN{} trajectories. A $3\times3$ grid over the $k$NN scale and anchor ($\kappa\in\{1,2,4\}$, $\tau\in\{1,2,4\}$) spans \SGridBestFullAlc--\SGridWorstFullAlc{} on a smaller synthetic generator, i.e.\ the kernel constants matter little.

\section{Fitting pipeline details}
\label{app:fit}

\texttt{fit\_prior\_from\_measurement\_db.py} runs in five steps, each a sub-command. \emph{plan}: list the repository, download the small \texttt{benchmarks} table of every directory and keep those with \texttt{response\_type = binary} and \texttt{granularity = item} (the organisers' eligibility rule in \texttt{tools/prepare\_data.py}); record the bytes still to download and stop above a cap (30 GB by default). \emph{download}: fetch the eligible \texttt{subjects}, \texttt{items} and \texttt{responses} tables into the organisers' payload layout. \emph{fit}: build one row per (subject, test condition, item) with the mean response over trials as label (fractional labels enter as two weighted rows), each pair receiving total weight one; compute the subject and item terms with the shipped \texttt{model.subject\_terms} and \texttt{model.item\_terms}, so that fitted coefficients map one-to-one onto the constants of Appendix~\ref{app:perpair}; add a one-hot for providers with at least 15 pairs and one for each training benchmark; fit an $L_2$-penalised logistic regression. Five-fold cross-validation holds out whole benchmarks; the penalty $C$ and the logit cap are selected by the pair-weighted budget-0 Brier score of the prior on held-out pairs, and $\alpha_0\in\{2,4,8\}$ by the closed-form shrinkage ALC of the protocol (50/50 split, 31 random acquisitions, Eq.~\ref{eq:alc}) on held-out pairs with at least 80 items, paired against the hand-set prior on the same pairs. \emph{testbeds}: evaluate the fitted table inside the harness on both local testbeds against the shipped constants. \emph{dry-run}: generate tables with the release schema (shared pool of subjects, random subsets per benchmark, benchmark difficulty offsets with standard deviation one logit, labels from a known coefficient table over the prior's own terms, two ineligible benchmarks) and run \emph{fit} on them. The dry run used \DryNBench{} eligible benchmarks, \DryNPairs{} pairs and \DryNRows{} rows; a light-regularisation fit recovers all \DryNSlopes{} slope signs with maximum error \DryMaxAbsErr{} and mean error \DryMeanAbsErr{} logits, and the fitted benchmark effects have standard deviation \DryBenchSd. Its cross-validated selection chose $C=\DrySelC$ and $\alpha_0=\DrySelAlpha$ and did not beat the hand-set prior (budget-0 Brier \DryPairBZeroMean{} [\DryPairBZeroLo, \DryPairBZeroHi]; shrinkage ALC \DryPairAlcMean{} [\DryPairAlcLo, \DryPairAlcHi]), so on these tables the rule would have kept the hand-set constants.

\paragraph{The run on the release.} Revision \texttt{\FitRevisionShort}; \FitNEligible{} eligible directories, \FitNBench{} with at least one pair of 20 or more distinct items; the pair unit is (benchmark directory, subject, test condition), since the release records several test conditions per subject on many benchmarks; \FitNPairs{} pairs, \FitNRows{} rows (each pair capped at \FitMaxItemsPerPair{} items); \FitNProviders{} providers with at least 15 pairs received a coefficient. Pair success rates have mean \FitRateMean{} and standard deviation \FitRateSd{} across pairs (benchmark means: standard deviation \FitBenchRateSd), while the hand-set $p_0$ has mean \FitHandPMean{} and standard deviation \FitHandPSd. The penalty grid ran from $C=\FitCMin$ to \FitCMax; the selection took $C=\FitSelC$, cap \FitSelCap{} and $\alpha_0=\FitSelAlpha$ (cross-validated budget-0 Brier \FitSelBZero, shrinkage ALC \FitSelAlc). The fitted benchmark effects have standard deviation \FitBenchEffectSd{} logits. Fitted vs.\ effective hand-set coefficients: release year \FitCoefYear{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefYear{} per year, size \FitCoefSize{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefSize{} per doubling, hard-tier cue \FitCoefTierHard{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefTierHard, easy-tier cue \FitCoefTierEasy{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefTierEasy, item length \FitCoefLength{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefLength; the reasoning-effort and harness terms shrink to about zero (\FitCoefEffortHigh, \FitCoefHarness; \FitFracPairsNoProvider{} of pairs carry no provider field and most carry no effort or harness field), and the provider table spans \FitProvMin{} (\texttt{\FitProvMinName}) to \FitProvMax{} (\texttt{\FitProvMaxName}). Inside the harness on the local testbeds the fitted table changes the full model's ALC by \FitRealPairFullMean{} [\FitRealPairFullLo, \FitRealPairFullHi] (real) and \FitSynPairFullMean{} [\FitSynPairFullLo, \FitSynPairFullHi] (synthetic); the result files are \texttt{prior\_fit.json} and \texttt{prior\_fit\_testbeds.json}, and the ship rule's verdict is recorded there (\FitShip).

\section{Item-text difficulty prior: details}
\label{app:textprior}

\paragraph{Pre-registration and outcome.} \texttt{experiments/results/round3/PREREG.md} (SHA-256 prefix \texttt{\TpPreregSha}, recorded in every result file and \TpPreregConsistent{} at build time) fixed features, target, model, nested selection, evaluation and ship rule before any text model was fitted. \texttt{PREREG\_ADDENDUM\_1.md} (\TpAddendumConsistent) registered the two exploratory variants after the primary folds had run, explicitly without influence on the decision. The rule: ship iff (a) the benchmark-held-out ALC difference (text minus hand-set) has a 95\% cluster-bootstrap CI below zero, (b) the real testbed is not significantly worse, and the engineering gates hold ($\ge200$ calls/s on one core with a new target item per call, weights $\le5$~MB, validator and smoke test pass, never raises). Outcome: (a) \TpRuleA, (b) \TpRuleB, gates \TpGates; the text prior is \TpShip.

\paragraph{Features and target.} JSON contents (the release stores many items as JSON objects) are flattened into \texttt{key: value} lines; words and word bigrams come from the first 2,000 characters (digit runs mapped to a length token), character trigrams from the first 500, and \texttt{item\_features} tokens form a fourth family; each family is sublinear-tf weighted and $\ell_2$-normalised, then hashed with \texttt{zlib.crc32} (sign from bit 31) into \TpBits{} buckets. The target of item $i$ is $\mathrm{logit}\,\frac{n_ir_i+0.5}{n_i+1}$ minus the mean hand-set subject logit of the $n_i$ pairs that evaluated it and minus its hand-set item logit, clipped to $\pm5$. At most 1,000 items per benchmark (\TpNTargetItems{} in total) are used for fitting; all \TpNItems{} items of the frame are scored (\TpNnz{} non-zeros). Exact duplicates of a test item's content in a training benchmark are rare (\TpDupMean\% of frame items on average), so shared items do not inflate the cross-validation.

\paragraph{Nested selection and fits.} In each outer fold a four-fold benchmark-grouped inner CV chooses the ridge penalty from $\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3,\TpAlphaGridMax\}$ by weighted MSE of the target; the inner out-of-fold scores give $W,B$ (pair-weighted logistic likelihood), the cap ($\{1.5,2.5,3.5\}$, training budget-0 Brier) and the within-pair weight $\lambda$ of the text offset ($\{0,0.5,1\}$, training closed-form ALC). Selected: penalty \TpFoldAlphas{} in every fold (the grid maximum), caps \TpFoldCaps, $\lambda=\TpFoldLambdas$, $W$ from \TpFoldWMin{} to \TpFoldWMax{} and $B$ from \TpFoldBMin{} to \TpFoldBMax. The held-out $R^2$ of the residual target was \TpFoldRsqMin{} to \TpFoldRsqMax{} across folds (inner CV: \TpInnerRsqMin{} to \TpInnerRsqMax). The final fit on all \TpNBench{} benchmarks again selected penalty \TpFinalAlpha{} with $W=\TpFinalW$, $B=\TpFinalB$, cap \TpFinalCap{} and $\lambda=\TpFinalLambda$; it is kept as \texttt{text\_prior\_candidate.json} (\TpFinalMB{} MB) and not shipped.

\label{app:frontier}

\paragraph{Discussion.}
The official test set has subjects our testbeds lack (2025--2026 releases, long reasoning efforts, very large models, agent harnesses). Appendix~\ref{app:frontier} tabulates the budget-0 prior over \SensNGrid{} such descriptions: $p_0$ ranges from \SensPMin{} to \SensPMax{} (the cap); the organisers' GPT-4o and 2026 medium-effort sample subjects receive \SensPSampleGptFourO{} and \SensPSmokeSubject, our testbed models \SensPTestbedQwenSmall--\SensPTestbedLlama. What a wrong prior costs is exact: with $\alpha_0=\MiscalAlpha$ and $n$ labels the expected Brier excess over the pair-rate oracle is $\mathbb E[(\rho_n-r)^2]$ with binomial successes (Table~\ref{tab:miscal}). A prior of $0.7$ on a pair whose true rate is $0.3$ costs \MiscalRThreePSeven{} ALC and loses to the constant (excess \MiscalConstRThreeExcess); shrinkage beats the constant whenever $|r-0.5|\ge0.2$ and $|p_0-r|\le0.3$, and never when $|r-0.5|<0.15$, where the constant is close to optimal. Shifting every subject's prior logit by $\pm0.5$ or $\pm1$ (Table~\ref{tab:offset}) is asymmetric on the real testbed: $+0.5$ and $+1$ cost \ROffPlusHalfFullVsZeroMean{} and \ROffPlusOneFullVsZeroMean{} ALC (both significant), whereas $-0.5$ changes it by \ROffMinusHalfFullVsZeroMean{} [\ROffMinusHalfFullVsZeroLo, \ROffMinusHalfFullVsZeroHi] (\ROffMinusHalfFullVsZeroSig)---small models at \RAccMin--\RAccMax\% accuracy tolerate a pessimistic prior; on the synthetic testbed zero is best and both $\pm0.5$ shifts cost significantly (\SOffMinusHalfFullVsZeroMean, \SOffPlusHalfFullVsZeroMean). On the training release the hand-set prior averages \FitHandPMean{} against a mean pair rate of \FitRateMean, i.e.\ it is not optimistic for real systems \emph{on average over the release}. Rounds 1--4 kept the intercept at zero and named it as the first constant to change if the official formative score pointed the other way. It did: on the hidden pool the budget-0 prior was worse than the constant, and the selected submission lowers the intercept and switches the subject terms off (Section~\ref{sec:why}).

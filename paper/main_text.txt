      Calibrate the Level, Borrow Across Pairs: A
    Random-Effects Predictor for the Predictive AI

                   Evaluation Competition

                                                   Neel Sirivara
                                            University of Washington
                                       nsiriv@cs.washington.edu

                                            Abstract

          We describe our selected submission to the Predictive AI Evaluation Competition
          (PAIEC), Codabench submission 1010717. It predicts whether an AI system
           answers a benchmark item correctly by fitting a random-effects logistic model,
           once per label-budget checkpoint, to the evaluator's global list of acquired labels:
           benchmark, subject and subject­benchmark effects, plus an exact-item effect
           learned from other subjects' labels on the same item. It differs from our previous
           submission (1010370) in three constants of the prior level only: the hand-set subject
           terms (release year, reasoning effort, model size, harness) are switched off, the prior
           logit is shifted by -0.5, and the prior standard deviation of the global level is raised
           from 0.5 to 2. The change came from the organisers' formative feedback: on the
           hidden test pool the previous budget-0 prior was worse than predicting 0.5 (Brier
           0.2834 against 0.25). In benchmark-held-out simulations of the official protocol on
           the training release, the cross-pair model changes the Brier area under the learning
           curve (ALC) by -0.0129 [-0.0151, -0.0109] relative to a per-pair predictor, and the
           new level changes it by -0.0069 to -0.0009 in harder, inventory-like populations and
           by +0.0018 to +0.0046 in release-like ones. Four formative runs, each a fresh draw
           of 8­9 subject­benchmark pairs on the same 7 hidden benchmarks, scored 0.1934
          (1010370), 0.1646, 0.1628 (1010717) and 0.1913. Every recalibrated run predicted
           budget 0 better than the constant, but the differences between runs are within draw
           noise (largest benchmark-matched |t| = 1.75). The predictor is standard-library
           Python. This work was done with an AI coding assistant (Claude).

1 Introduction

Evaluating every new AI system on every benchmark is expensive, and the number of benchmarks
keeps growing [Liang et al., 2023]. Recent work therefore predicts performance instead of measuring
it: from a few informative items selected with item response theory (IRT) [Polo et al., 2024, Kipnis
et al., 2025, Perlitz et al., 2024], adaptively per model [Hofmann et al., 2025, Zhuang et al., 2025],
from other models' scores [Ruan et al., 2024, Ye et al., 2023], or from a few labelled instances of the
new model [Pacchiardi et al., 2024]. PAIEC [Stanford AIMS Lab, 2026] makes this a forecasting task
with a fixed interface: predict([subject, item], labeled) returns Pr(correct) for an item of
a benchmark from the private test pool, where labeled holds the labels acquired so far, at budgets
of n  {0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31} labels per subject­benchmark pair.

Our selected submission (1010717) has two parts. Cross-pair pooling (Section 3): the evaluator
passes one list with the labels of all sampled pairs to every prediction, and a random-effects model
fitted to that list learns benchmark difficulty from other subjects and item difficulty from other
subjects' labels on the same item. A recalibrated budget-0 level (Section 4): at budget 0 the list is

Contributions. (1) A cross-pair random-effects predictor for the evaluator's global label list,
selected by pre-registered, benchmark-held-out simulation of the official protocol on the training
release (Sections 3, 5.2). (2) A diagnosis of the budget-0 prior from formative feedback, and a

honest reading of four formative runs: what they show (budget 0) and what they cannot show (which
of the variants is better), with run-to-run noise quantified (Section 5.1). (4) Exact rerun instructions
and a generated, audited report: every number is produced from result files by a script (Section 7).
Earlier rounds and negative results are in the appendices.

Related work. Item response models, with a subject ability and an item difficulty on a logit scale,
are widely used for LLM benchmark data, to rank systems, find informative items and estimate
full-benchmark scores from few items [Rodriguez et al., 2021, Polo et al., 2024, Kipnis et al., 2025].
They fit item parameters offline on hundreds of models. In PAIEC the test benchmarks are new,
so difficulty must be learned during evaluation from a few labels of a few subjects. We keep the
Rasch structure [Lord, 1980] but replace free parameters by random effects with Gaussian priors,
a generalised linear mixed model [Breslow and Clayton, 1993]: a benchmark's difficulty is shrunk
towards the prior when it has one or three labels and is learned when it has hundreds. Work that
predicts a model's scores from other models' scores [Ruan et al., 2024, Ye et al., 2023] motivates
pooling across subjects; our model does this at the item level, inside one evaluation run.

2 Task, data and the evaluation interface

Task and metric. A subject is a dictionary of strings (normalized_name, provider,
release_date, access_date, harness, harness_version, reasoning_effort,
subject_features_extra). An item has item_content, item_features, interactors
and an anonymous benchmark_id. Each subject­benchmark pair with at least 80 distinct items

is split 50/50 into an acquisition and an evaluation pool, fixed across submissions. Acquisition
candidates arrive in a fixed random order. An optional acquisition_function returns True to

reveal a label (at most 31 per pair); otherwise labels are drawn at random. After 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31

labels per pair, every evaluation item is predicted, giving Brier scores [Brier, 1950] B0, . . . , B31 and

ALC = 0.1 B0 + 0.2 B1 + 0.2 B3 + 0.2 B7 + 0.2 B15 + 0.1 B31,                        (1)

averaged over pairs (lower is better). Predicting 0.5 gives Bn = 0.25 on any data.

The global label list. In the organisers' streaming client (tools/streaming_ingestion.py,
run_streaming), all predictions of a checkpoint receive the same labeled list, and the competition
page states that it "contains [[subject, item], label] entries across sampled subject­benchmark pairs".
At budget n the list holds up to n labels of every pair; at budget 0 it is empty. The rules allow this

evidence: "the submitted program may use the attributes and acquired labels supplied through the

evaluation interface to adapt during evaluation". The model uses only anonymous keys (the full
attribute dictionary, benchmark_id, item text) inside one run and keeps nothing across runs.

Data. Training release. The official aims-foundations/measurement-db (revision
54074453da) gives 16,718 pairs of (subject, test condition) on 144 benchmarks with at least 20
items (16,303 with 80), 3,118,742 item-level labels (mean over trials) and 165,764 distinct items.
We use it only to simulate the protocol and to select a few constants; no regression coefficient of
the submission is fitted to it. It differs from the hidden population in ways that matter for the prior:
92.7% of pairs share (model name, benchmark) with another pair, 58% of eligible pairs have an
empty normalized_name, and the 33 release benchmarks that are rows of the organisers' benchmark
inventory are harder than the rest (mean pair success rate 0.466 vs. 0.547). The organisers' curation
guidelines restrict competition-eligible base models to 2025­2026 releases [Stanford AIMS Lab,
2026]. Hidden test pool. Our four formative runs (Section 5.1) each scored 8­9 pairs with 491­498
evaluation subject­items, always on the same 7 benchmark IDs; across runs 35 distinct subject IDs

3 The submitted predictor

Budget-0 prior. The prior logit is o(s, i) = clip  zsubj(s) +  + zitem(i), ±1.5 , and at budget 0
the prediction is (o). The item part zitem reads tier, level and points cues in item_features and
the item length; the hand-set subject part zsubj rewards a recent release date, a high reasoning effort,
a large parameter count or size word in the name, and a non-default harness (Appendix D). The
selected submission sets  = SUBJECT_SHRINK = 0 and  = PRIOR_INTERCEPT = -0.5 (Table 1):
every subject starts from the same level, so an item without cues receives (-0.5) = 0.38, and only

item cues move the prior.

Cross-pair random-effects model. From budget 1 on, for a label ye of subject s on item i of
benchmark b in the global list,

             logit Pr(ye = 1) = o(s, i) + µ + vb + us + wsb,                     (2)

with independent priors µ  N (0, µ2), vb  N (0, b2), us  N (0, s2), wsb  N (0, s2b). Sub-
jects are identified by their complete attribute dictionary and benchmarks by benchmark_id.
The MAP estimate is computed by backfitting: one Newton step per effect type and sweep,
g  g + eg(ye - pe) - g/ 2 / eg pe(1 - pe) + 1/ 2 , at most 25 sweeps (penalised
quasi-likelihood [Breslow and Clayton, 1993]). The target's latent logit m = o + µ^ + v^b + u^s + w^sb
(zero for groups without labels) has approximate posterior variance V = g 1/(Hg + g-2) (g2

for an unseen group), and p =  m/ 1 + V /8 [MacKay, 1992]. Exact-item effect. If the target

item (same benchmark_id and text) was labelled for other subjects, an item effect d  N (0, d2) is
estimated from those labels by 4 Newton steps on the conditional scale (their stage-1 logits multiplied
by c = 1 + d2/8), and p =  (c m + d^)/ 1 +  Var(d)/8 with m the probit-scaled stage-1
logit. Prior standard deviations: b = 1.5, s = 0.5, sb = 0.6, d = 2.25, selected in round 4
(Section 5.2); µ = 2 in the selected submission (0.5 before). With the prior level lowered, a wide µ
lets the global level be set by the labels from budget 1 on rather than held near the prior.

Acquisition: fresh first. A label on an item nobody has labelled can help every subject whose
evaluation pool contains that item. labeling.py accepts candidates in stream order (a uniformly

random sample, as the default policy) but skips a candidate whose item is already in the global list for

another subject while labels_remaining < items_remaining, so the budget is always spent. It takes
no prediction argument, so the client does not call predict during acquisition.

Implementation. model.py and labeling.py use only the Python standard library, need no
data files, models or network, and are deterministic. Because labeled is identical for all calls of
a checkpoint, the parsed list and the fit are cached at module level under a fingerprint and replaced

atomically under a lock. For the selected files, a 62,000-label list is fitted in 1.79 s and a cached call

takes 0.126 ms on one laptop core (Table 10 for other sizes). Any failure falls back to the per-pair

predictor of our earlier rounds (Appendix D), then to the prior, then to 0.5.

4 Why recalibrate the budget-0 level

What the formative feedback showed. Codabench returns, per run, the Brier ALC, the ECE and
the per-budget scores of every pair (anonymous IDs). We use only aggregates over pairs. For our
previous submission 1010370, budget 0 scored B0 = 0.2834, above the constant's 0.25. Since 0.25
is the constant's score on any draw, this is a comparison within one draw. The budget-0 ECE was
0.340, above the 0.235­0.299 that the same predictor shows in all four simulated populations of
Appendix A. From n = 1 on, the cross-pair model learns the level from the list, and the later budgets
were in the range of the simulated inventory population (B1 = 0.2103 and B3 = 0.1909 against
0.2048 and 0.1893). Two facts about the training release point the same way. On the 33 inventory
benchmarks, the hand-set prior is already worse than the constant at budget 0 (B0  0.2596; 0.2653
for subjects released in 2025 or later). And the release-year term raises the prior of every eligible test
subject, since all are 2025­2026 releases, which is the wrong direction if the hidden benchmarks are
harder than the release. A table of subject ability by model name, learned from the release, does not
transfer to held-out benchmarks either (correlation -0.06 to +0.10, R2  0.019; Appendix B). So the
subject terms are removed rather than re-fitted.

Simulated populations. We do not know the hidden pool's composition, so we compare the
variants in seven populations built from the training release with the round-4 simulator (Section 5.2;
formative-sized test sets of up to ten pairs on one to three benchmarks, benchmark-held-out). They
range from release-like (A, and Y25 with only 2025+ subjects) to inventory-like (INV, INV25) and
harder (MID, HARD), plus SHIFT, in which every prior offset is one logit too high (Table 2). The
level matters almost only at budget 0: from n = 1 on, the global and benchmark effects take the level
from the labels, so no later budget changes by more than 0.0046 in any population.

The choice. Table 2. Switching the subject terms off alone (1010715) changes the ALC by only
-0.0006 to -0.0019 outside release-like data. Adding the -0.5 shift (1010717) changes it by -0.0041
[-0.0057, -0.0025] on MID, -0.0069 [-0.0090, -0.0048] on HARD and -0.0042 on SHIFT; it is not
significantly different on INV (-0.0020) and INV25 (-0.0009) and costs +0.0018 [+0.0005, +0.0032]
on A and +0.0046 on Y25. A shift of -1 (1010735) changes the ALC relative to 1010717 by -0.0018
to +0.0027 in those five populations but more than doubles the release-like cost (+0.0057 on A,
+0.0104 on Y25). Because the feedback indicates that, at budget 0, the hidden pool does not behave
like the release, we chose 1010717: significant gains in 3 of the five non-release-like populations, no
significant loss in any of them, and a bounded cost if we are wrong. The choice was recorded in our
deep-dive notes (DEEP_DIVE.md) while the candidates' formative runs were pending, together with a
rule for revising it: switch only if the selected run looked release-like at budget 0 and its budget-0
Brier, matched by benchmark, were worse than the previous submission's. It was better (-0.0446, se
0.0644), so the choice stood.

5 Results

5.1 Formative runs on the hidden pool

Budget 0. Table 3, Figure 1. The previous submission was the only run whose budget-0 Brier
exceeded the constant's 0.25; the three recalibrated variants scored 0.2247­0.2458, and their budget-0
ECE was lower (0.278 for 1010717). This is the effect the change targets, and it is a within-draw
comparison against a fixed reference. It is still one draw per variant.

The rest is within draw noise. The ALCs of the four runs span 0.1628­0.1934 (standard deviation
0.017). The simulator expects the three constants to change the ALC by -0.0069 to +0.0046 and any
budget n  1 by at most 0.0046; yet the raw difference 1010717 - 1010370 averages -0.027 over
n  1. Those differences come from the draws, not from the constants. A fairer comparison matches
runs by benchmark, which the anonymous IDs allow without identifying anything: averaged over
the 7 shared benchmarks, 1010717 - 1010370 is -0.0293 ALC (se 0.0284). The standard errors of
such differences are 0.024­0.041, and none of the six pairwise comparisons reaches two of them
(largest |t| = 1.75 with 6 degrees of freedom; Appendix A). The four runs therefore do not show that
1010717 has a lower expected ALC than 1010370 or than its siblings, and we do not claim it; they
show only that the recalibrated prior beat the constant at budget 0 on each of its draws. The final
ranking uses one common test subset for all teams, where only the expected score matters.

Leaderboard context. The public board for this phase (snapshot 2026-10-26) has 58 entries: #1
0.1172, #10 0.1483, median 0.1768; the entry of the organisers' account (AIMS Foundations) scores
0.1801. Each entry is a user's best draw, and up to 50 uploads per day are allowed, so board positions
mix skill and selection. Our selected run (0.1628) would sit at about place 22, our previous one
(0.1934) at 46. Even our best draw is far from the leaders; in simulation, most of the remaining
headroom is item-level difficulty (Appendix B).

5.2 Development evidence for the cross-pair model

Protocol. The round-4 simulator rebuilds the official protocol on the training release with five
benchmark-held-out folds. A test set is a set of pairs of one fold sharing one global list. Per
pair, items are split 50/50 and the acquisition pool is permuted; at budget n every prediction of
the test set receives the union of all its pairs' first n labels. The primary configuration A draws
1­3 random benchmarks and 10 pairs with 100 items each (1,000 subject­item pairs, as in for-
mative runs), 300 test sets per fold. Configurations B­E vary the composition (Appendix C).
The design, the grid of 864 prior settings, nested selection and a ship rule were pre-registered
(experiments/results/round4/PREREG.md, SHA-256 prefix b9e95a5b47e9) before any out-
come was computed. 95% CIs come from a cluster bootstrap over benchmarks (2,000 resamples).

Cross-pair gain. Table 4. In configuration A the cross-pair model reaches 0.1966 against 0.2095
for the per-pair predictor (-0.0129 [-0.0151, -0.0109], lower on 73% of units), closing 35% of the gap
to the pair-rate oracle. The gain holds when cross-pair evidence only comes from different models
(E: -0.0125 [-0.0148, -0.0100]) and grows when more pairs share the list (B: -0.0289; C: -0.0393).
Removing the benchmark effect costs +0.0048 [+0.0032, +0.0064] and removing the exact-item
effect +0.0048 [+0.0038, +0.0059]: benchmark difficulty, invisible at budget 0, and item difficulty,
invisible from a pair's own 31 labels, contribute about equally (Appendix C). The fresh-first rule
changes the ALC by -0.0005 [-0.0009, -0.0002], almost all at n  15.

Inside the organisers' client. Driven by a mock coordinator on the local testbed, the organisers'
run_streaming runs the selected files in stream acquisition mode with 0 predict calls during
acquisition and 31 labels on each of the 17 pairs. The ALC is 0.2179 against 0.2179 for the previous
files (+0.00003): the subjects there are small 2024 models, and the level change hardly matters.
The selected model.py reproduces the vectorised simulator with the same constants to 1.1 × 10-5
on 56,750 predictions. The organisers' check_submission_zip.py passes on the ZIP, and our
smoke test (44 interface and robustness cases plus 220 cross-pair cases) passes. On the local testbed
the cross-pair model's ALC difference to our earlier predictor is -0.0074 [-0.0203, +0.0032] (not
significant; significant in 4 of 5 acquisition trajectories).

6 Limitations

The choice rests on simulation and one budget-0 observation. The hidden pool's composition is
unknown. The seven populations bracket it, but they are all built from the training release, whose
subjects (many test-condition variants of one model, many without names or release dates) differ from
the 2025­2026 competition subjects. If the hidden pool were release-like, 1010717 would cost about
+0.0018­+0.0046 ALC. Formative noise. Each formative run is one draw of 8­9 pairs; four runs
cannot rank the variants (Section 5.1). We did not estimate how often the previous prior would exceed
0.25 at budget 0 on a release-like draw. Choosing the level after seeing formative feedback is a mild
form of tuning on the test pool; we changed three constants once, on the strength of one aggregate and
the simulations. The level is global. One intercept cannot fix benchmark-specific difficulty at budget
0. In simulation, knowing each test benchmark's mean difficulty would change the ALC by -0.0065
(release-like) to -0.0114 (hard), and knowing item difficulty by -0.0417 to -0.0342 (Appendix B).
Nothing in the submission estimates either before labels arrive. Cross-pair assumptions. The gain
depends on how many pairs share a list; item identity is exact text match within a benchmark; pairs
with fewer than 80 items are handled under one assumed rule (Appendix C). Local testbed. 17 pairs

Budget-0 prior. A logistic prior p0 = (z) with z = clip(zsubj +zitem, ±1.5), so p0  [0.18, 0.82].
The subject part is zsubj = 0.7 · (0.35 clip(t - 2024.5, ±2) + e + 0.25 clip(log2(N/30B), ±4) +
0.1 [non-default harness]), where t is the release year (fractional), e  {0, 0.15, 0.3, 0.45} encodes
an empty, low, other or high reasoning_effort, and N is a parameter count parsed from the name
("7b", "70B"); when no count is present, name tokens such as mini/nano/flash contribute -0.3 and

pro/opus/ultra contribute 0.2. A per-provider table (0 entries, inside the multiplier) and an intercept
 added after it (PRIOR_INTERCEPT: 0 in round 4, -0.5 in the selected submission) complete the

subject part; the provider table is empty and is the slot a fitted coefficient table would fill. The item
part parses key=value pairs in item_features: a tier/difficulty/level value matching hard/expert/. . .
adds -0.6, easy/basic/. . . adds 0.5, a numeric level   [1, 5] adds -0.25( - 3), and points above

one subtract 0.1 each (capped at five); finally the item length adds -0.12 clip(log2(L/600), ±3) for
L characters. The directions encode common knowledge (newer, larger, reasoning-enabled subjects

do better; harder, longer, higher-value items are failed more often); the magnitudes were chosen to

move the prior by at most a few tenths of a logit per attribute and were not tuned on either testbed.

Pair-level shrinkage. Given n acquired labels y1, . . . , yn of the same pair with item logits zi(tje)m
and mean z¯, the prior rate is r = (clip(zsubj + z¯)) and the posterior mean of a Beta­binomial model
with prior strength 0 = 4 pseudo-counts is  = (0r + j yj)/(0 + n) [Efron and Morris, 1975].
The pair estimate for a target item is ppair = (logit  + zitem - z¯), i.e. the item cues shift the pair
rate on the logit scale.

Item-level component 1: ridge features. From n  7 labels, three cheap features of the item

text--log length, digit density and log(1+number of sentences)--are standardised over the acquired
items and a logistic regression with offset logit ppair and ridge penalty  = 4 is fitted by six Newton
steps; its adjustment clip(f~, ±1) is added to the target's logit.

Item-level component 2: TF-IDF nearest neighbours. Item texts (content, features and interactors,
truncated to 4000 characters) are tokenised into lower-case words and numbers; the acquired set
defines the vocabulary and document frequencies, with sublinear term frequency, smoothed idf and
2 normalisation (bigram weight 1; target terms absent from the acquired vocabulary are dropped:
no). With cosine similarities sj between the target and acquired item j and weights wj = (sj)2, the
correction is a shrunken kernel-weighted residual,

p = clip a + n · j wj (yj - a) ,  N0 = 4,  = 2,  (3)
                 n + N0  + j wj

where the anchor a is the pair estimate (optionally including the ridge adjustment) and  = 2 by
default. With at most 31 short documents most bigrams are unique, so cosine similarities are small
(typically below 0.15 even for related items), which deliberately keeps the correction small unless
items share many terms; Appendix G compares a sharper unigram-only representation.

Leave-one-out gating. From n  5 labels, every configuration in {ridge off, on} × { 
{0, 1, 2, 4}} ( = 0 means no kNN) is scored by its leave-one-out Brier score on the acquired
labels themselves: label j is predicted from the other n - 1 labels, including a leave-one-out shrink-

age estimate and a leave-one-out ridge fit. The best configuration replaces plain shrinkage only if its
mean per-item improvement exceeds 2 standard error(s) of that mean; otherwise the predictor returns
ppair. This makes adaptation opt-in per pair and budget: a pair whose labels show no exploitable
structure is predicted by shrinkage alone. Below 5 labels no item-level component is used at all (in
preliminary runs an always-on kNN from one or three labels only added noise at B1 and B3).

E Rounds 1­3: data, testbeds and the training release

What we could and could not use. The official training data (measurement-db: item-level
responses of many subjects on curated benchmarks) require accepting terms with a Hugging Face

account. For most of the project we had no credentials and did not try to obtain the data in any other

way; the per-pair predictor and all its constants were fixed before any of it was seen. Its public file
listing (2026-10-22, revision 54074453da) held 281 benchmark directories: 14.8 GB of benchmarks,
subjects, items and responses tables plus 107.8 GB of traces and 154.7 GB of assets. On that
day the author's login became available and we downloaded the 163 competition-eligible directories
(binary, item-level responses; the eligibility flags live inside the benchmarks tables) without traces
or assets, and used them as described next. We also inspected the organisers' inventory of eligible

benchmarks (1,261 entries) but fitted nothing on any of them. The two local testbeds below only

evaluate design choices; the few constants they influenced are marked in Appendix D and probed in

Appendix F.

The training release:  validation and a fit that was not shipped.

fit_prior_from_measurement_db.py builds one row per (subject, test condition, item)

with the mean response over trials as label--16,718 pairs with at least 20 distinct items from 144

Real Ollama testbed. To obtain item-level correctness labels of real language models we ran
four small open-weight models locally with Ollama (Q4_K_M quantisation, greedy decoding, CPU
only): Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B, 3B and 7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Three public benchmarks
were used, none of which appears in the PAIEC inventory (only a derived variant, mr_gsm8k, does):
the first 200 GSM8K test items, the first 200 SVAMP test items (with the SVAMP problem type
as item_features), and a 300-item mix of ten BIG-Bench-Hard subtasks (30 items each, subtask
name as item_features). Two harnesses are represented: a direct-answer prompt ("reply with only
the final answer"; exact match after numeric or option normalisation) and, for GSM8K/SVAMP, an
earlier 8-shot chain-of-thought run of the Qwen models whose greedy answers we re-used. Each
(model, harness, benchmark) triple with at least 80 items is one pair, giving 17 pairs, 3,800 labelled
items, and success rates from 8.0% to 79.0% (Table 17 in the appendix). The subject dictionaries
carry the real model names, providers, release dates and harness names, so the budget-0 prior sees
realistic inputs; the raw records are released with the code.

Synthetic IRT testbed. Because the real testbed is small (small models, three benchmarks), we
complement it with a generator that follows a two-parameter item response model [Lord, 1980,
Embretson and Reise, 2000]. Subject s has ability s correlated with its visible attributes (release
year, reasoning effort, parameter count) plus noise; benchmark b has a location µb, a discrimination ab
and K = 5 topic clusters with difficulty offsets ck  N (0, t2opic); item i in cluster k(i) has difficulty
bi = µb + ck(i) +  zlen(i) + i and Pr(ysi = 1) = (ab(s - bi)). Item texts mix cluster-specific
vocabulary (35% of tokens), benchmark template words, ambient vocabulary and numbers, so that
lexical similarity carries difficulty information exactly when topic > 0; half of the benchmarks
expose a tier=easy/medium/hard feature derived from difficulty terciles. The main setting uses
10 subjects × 16 benchmarks = 160 pairs with 100­240 items each, topic = 0.70 and  = 0.3; a
sweep varies both. Because the true probabilities are known, we can report two oracle references: the
Brier score of the true item probabilities (the floor of any predictor) and that of the evaluation-set
success rate used as a constant (the floor of any predictor without item-level information).

Headroom. The oracle rows explain why. On the synthetic testbed, knowing the true probability of
every item gives an ALC of 0.1379, knowing only the evaluation-set success rate 0.1598: perfect

When does text help? The synthetic sweep (Figure 4, right; Table 19) varies how much difficulty

Gating. Replacing the LOO gate by always-on components at their default settings gives 0.1808

Sentence embeddings. The embeddings were computed offline for the real testbed (the model is
on the validator's allow-list via models.txt, so it could have been shipped). They change the full
model's ALC from 0.2224 to 0.2223 (paired -0.0001 [-0.0005, +0.0001], not significant) and the
always-on kNN variant from 0.2226 to 0.2252 (+0.0026 [+0.0000, +0.0055], significant), at the cost
of a neural dependency, model loading and about 10 ms per embedding on CPU.

Sharper lexical similarity does not help. A unigram-only TF-IDF whose target terms are restricted
to the acquired vocabulary separates same-topic from other-topic synthetic items far better (mean
cosine about 0.3 versus 0.1 in the generator) but makes the always-on kNN worse: paired against
the shipped representation, the always-on kNN changes by +0.0009 [-0.0001, +0.0020] (synthetic)
and +0.0026 [+0.0006, +0.0050] (real), and the gated full model by +0.0004 [+0.0001, +0.0008] and
+0.0006 [-0.0002, +0.0018]. With a handful of neighbours per topic the kernel-weighted residual is
dominated by label noise; the bigram-diluted similarity acts as a regulariser. Item-level signal exists
(the oracles show it) but 31 labels are too few to exploit it through similarity alone.

streams. Plain stream order differs from the evaluator's random policy by -0.0013 [-0.0038, +0.0013]
(synthetic, not significant) and -0.0044 [-0.0107, +0.0019] (real, not significant)--both are uniform

that similar--yet its ALC equals that of plain stream order to four decimals (0.2180 vs. 0.2180):
skipping a near-duplicate changes which uniformly random items are labelled, not their distribution.
The organisers' uncertainty band gives +0.0001 [-0.0027, +0.0030] and -0.0041 [-0.0096, +0.0016],
and the novelty-plus-uncertainty variant -0.0013 and -0.0044: selecting by |p - 0.5| reorders a
near-random sample at small budgets and, once item-level corrections exist, biases it towards items
of intermediate predicted difficulty, distorting the pair-rate estimate that carries most of the metric.
None of the policies is significantly different from random on either testbed; we ship the one that is
closest to random while removing redundant items.

I Fitting pipeline details

fit_prior_from_measurement_db.py runs in five steps, each a sub-command. plan: list
the repository, download the small benchmarks table of every directory and keep those
with response_type = binary and granularity = item (the organisers' eligibility rule in
tools/prepare_data.py); record the bytes still to download and stop above a cap (30 GB by
default). download: fetch the eligible subjects, items and responses tables into the organisers'
payload layout. fit: build one row per (subject, test condition, item) with the mean response over trials
as label (fractional labels enter as two weighted rows), each pair receiving total weight one; compute
the subject and item terms with the shipped model.subject_terms and model.item_terms, so
that fitted coefficients map one-to-one onto the constants of Appendix D; add a one-hot for providers
with at least 15 pairs and one for each training benchmark; fit an L2-penalised logistic regression.
Five-fold cross-validation holds out whole benchmarks; the penalty C and the logit cap are selected
by the pair-weighted budget-0 Brier score of the prior on held-out pairs, and 0  {2, 4, 8} by the
closed-form shrinkage ALC of the protocol (50/50 split, 31 random acquisitions, Eq. 1) on held-out
pairs with at least 80 items, paired against the hand-set prior on the same pairs. testbeds: evaluate the
fitted table inside the harness on both local testbeds against the shipped constants. dry-run: generate
tables with the release schema (shared pool of subjects, random subsets per benchmark, benchmark
difficulty offsets with standard deviation one logit, labels from a known coefficient table over the
prior's own terms, two ineligible benchmarks) and run fit on them. The dry run used 14 eligible
benchmarks, 299 pairs and 47,784 rows; a light-regularisation fit recovers all 13 slope signs with
maximum error 0.05 and mean error 0.02 logits, and the fitted benchmark effects have standard devia-
tion 0.57. Its cross-validated selection chose C = 0.003 and 0 = 4 and did not beat the hand-set
prior (budget-0 Brier +0.0036 [+0.0004, +0.0069]; shrinkage ALC +0.0013 [+0.0001, +0.0027]), so
on these tables the rule would have kept the hand-set constants.

The run on the release. Revision 54074453da; 163 eligible directories, 144 with at least one pair
of 20 or more distinct items; the pair unit is (benchmark directory, subject, test condition), since the
release records several test conditions per subject on many benchmarks; 16,718 pairs, 3,118,742
rows (each pair capped at 200 items); 22 providers with at least 15 pairs received a coefficient. Pair

K Prior sensitivity for frontier-like subjects

Table 13 gives the budget-0 prior p0 of the round-4 constants (1010370; in the selected submission p0
no longer depends on the subject) for subject descriptions that the official test set is likely to contain
and our testbeds do not. Table 14 gives the exact expected cost of a miscalibrated prior under the
per-pair shrinkage rule, and Table 15 the measured effect of shifting every subject's prior logit on the
two testbeds (PRIOR_INTERCEPT in model.py; all other constants unchanged; identical trajectories;
generated by prior_sensitivity.py).

Discussion. The official test set has subjects our testbeds lack (2025­2026 releases, long reasoning
efforts, very large models, agent harnesses). Appendix K tabulates the budget-0 prior over 160 such
descriptions: p0 ranges from 0.39 to 0.82 (the cap); the organisers' GPT-4o and 2026 medium-effort
sample subjects receive 0.49 and 0.66, our testbed models 0.36­0.39. What a wrong prior costs is
exact: with 0 = 4 and n labels the expected Brier excess over the pair-rate oracle is E[(n - r)2]
with binomial successes (Table 14). A prior of 0.7 on a pair whose true rate is 0.3 costs 0.062 ALC
and loses to the constant (excess 0.040); shrinkage beats the constant whenever |r - 0.5|  0.2 and
|p0 - r|  0.3, and never when |r - 0.5| < 0.15, where the constant is close to optimal. Shifting
every subject's prior logit by ±0.5 or ±1 (Table 15) is asymmetric on the real testbed: +0.5 and +1
cost +0.0134 and +0.0353 ALC (both significant), whereas -0.5 changes it by -0.0034 [-0.0117,
+0.0049] (not significant)--small models at 8.0­79.0% accuracy tolerate a pessimistic prior; on the
synthetic testbed zero is best and both ±0.5 shifts cost significantly (+0.0037, +0.0047). On the
training release the hand-set prior averages 0.510 against a mean pair rate of 0.537, i.e. it is not
optimistic for real systems on average over the release. Rounds 1­4 kept the intercept at zero and
named it as the first constant to change if the official formative score pointed the other way. It did: on

the intercept and switches the subject terms off (Section 4).

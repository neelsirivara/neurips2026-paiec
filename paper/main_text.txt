                                            Abstract

          The Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC) asks for the probability that an
          AI system (the subject) answers a benchmark item correctly, given the subject's
           visible attributes, the item text, and between 0 and 31 labels acquired on the same
           subject­benchmark pair. Scoring is the Brier area-under-the-learning-curve (ALC),
          which places 60% of its weight on budgets of at most seven labels. We describe
           a submission built, for most of the project, without the official training release
          (measurement-db is gated; credentials became available two days before the
           deadline), i.e. a predictor with no fitted parameters: a hand-set attribute prior at
           budget zero, beta-binomial shrinkage of the pair success rate towards that prior,
           and two item-level adaptation components--a ridge-logistic adjustment on cheap
           item features and a TF-IDF nearest-neighbour correction--that are switched on
           only when a leave-one-out test on the acquired labels shows a clear improvement.
          A streaming acquisition policy removes near-duplicate candidates. Because the
           test data are private, we reproduce the official protocol locally on a real testbed
           of 17 pairs (four small open models on GSM8K, SVAMP and BIG-Bench-Hard)
           and on a synthetic IRT testbed with oracle bounds: the shipped predictor reaches a
           Brier ALC of 0.2224 (95% CI 0.1949­0.2454) against 0.2871 for the organisers'
           empirical-mean baseline and 0.25 for the constant, and 0.1797 against 0.2208
           on the synthetic testbed. Oracle analysis splits the remaining headroom into rate
           estimation at small budgets, which only a better prior can reduce, and item-level
           discrimination, which 31 labels cannot deliver: the gated item-level components
           change the ALC by +0.0000 (synthetic) and -0.0004 (real), sentence embeddings
           did not beat TF-IDF, and uncertainty sampling did not beat random acquisition.
          When the release became available we used it three times: to validate the shipped
           rule on 16,303 real subject­benchmark pairs from 144 benchmarks with the compe-
           tition protocol in closed form (ALC 0.2051 against 0.2396 for the empirical mean
           and 0.2470 for the constant), to fit the attribute prior, and, under a pre-registered
           ship rule, to train an item-text difficulty prior on other benchmarks' items. Under
           benchmark-held-out cross-validation neither beat the hand-set prior (text prior:
          ALC change -0.0000 [-0.0013, +0.0014]; its out-of-fold slope was negative in
           every fold), so nothing fitted is shipped. The predictor is pure standard-library
           Python, deterministic, and runs at about 1,010 calls per second on one CPU core;
           the submission ZIP passes the organisers' validator. Code and text were produced
          with the help of an LLM coding assistant (Claude).

1 Introduction

Evaluating a new AI system on the full set of available benchmarks is expensive, and the number
of benchmarks keeps growing [Liang et al., 2023]. A line of recent work therefore asks how well
performance can be predicted rather than measured: from a few hundred carefully chosen items [Polo
et al., 2024, Perlitz et al., 2024], from the scores of other models [Ruan et al., 2024], or from a handful
of labelled instances of the new model itself [Pacchiardi et al., 2024]. PAIEC [Stanford AIMS Lab,
2026] turns this into a forecasting task with a precise interface: a function predict([subject,
item], labeled) must return Pr(correct) for one subject­item pair, where labeled contains the
labels already acquired on the same subject­benchmark pair at budgets n  {0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31}. Pairs
are scored by the Brier score [Brier, 1950] at each budget and aggregated into a Brier ALC with
weights (0.1, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.1)--the trapezoidal area under the Brier-versus-log2(n+1) curve.

Two facts about this metric shaped our design. First, 60% of the weight sits at n  7, where only the
pair-level success rate can be estimated with any precision; item-level discrimination can pay off only
at n = 15 and n = 31, which together carry 30%. Second, the organisers' empirical-mean baseline is
worse than the constant 0.5 at n = 1 (its prediction is 0 or 1), so the single most valuable ingredient
is a well-shrunk estimate of the pair rate. Our predictor therefore shrinks first: an attribute-based
prior at n = 0, a beta-binomial posterior mean of the pair rate for n  1 [Efron and Morris, 1975],
and only then item-level adaptation, which is gated by a leave-one-out (LOO) test on the acquired
labels so that it is applied only when the pair's own labels show that it helps.

We also faced a constraint that defines the submission: the official training release
(aims-foundations/measurement-db) is gated behind a Hugging Face login, and for most of the
project the machine used for this work had no credentials (we did not create any account; the author's
login arrived two days before the deadline). The predictor therefore contains no parameter fitted
on any response data: every constant is a documented prior (Section 4), and the report tests their
sensitivity, including for frontier-like subjects. Section 3 describes what the release was then used for:
validating the shipped rule on real pairs, a cross-validated fit of the prior that lost to the hand-set one,
and a pre-registered item-text difficulty prior that did not transfer to held-out benchmarks; neither was
shipped. We see this as a strong, fully reproducible baseline for the competition and as a measurement
of how far test-time adaptation alone can go.

Contributions. (1) A standard-library-only predictor and streaming acquisition policy that pass the
official validator and run at hundreds of calls per second. (2) A local reproduction of the evaluation
protocol on a real testbed of 17 pairs built with local open models and on a synthetic IRT testbed with
known item probabilities, plus a validation of the shipped rule on 16,303 pairs of the official training
release. (3) A paired-bootstrap analysis of what helps (prior, shrinkage), what helps marginally
(item-level components) and what does not (sentence embeddings, uncertainty sampling, a fitted
attribute prior, and a pre-registered item-text difficulty prior trained on 144 other benchmarks), with
oracle bounds that locate the remaining headroom.

2 Task, interface and metric

A subject is a dictionary of strings (normalized_name, provider, release_date, access_date,
harness, harness_version, reasoning_effort, subject_features_extra); an item has
item_content (the task text), item_features (e.g. tier=medium;points=1), interactors,
and an anonymous benchmark_id shared by all items of a benchmark. Missing values are empty

strings. For every subject­benchmark pair with at least 80 distinct items, the evaluator splits the items

50/50 into an acquisition pool (never scored) and an evaluation pool. Acquisition candidates arrive
in a fixed random order; an optional acquisition_function(input, prediction, labeled,
context) returns True to reveal a label (31 at most), otherwise labels are sampled uniformly. The
nested label sets after 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 reveals are passed to predict for every evaluation item,

giving Brier scores B0, . . . , B31 and

ALC = 0.1 B0 + 0.2 B1 + 0.2 B3 + 0.2 B7 + 0.2 B15 + 0.1 B31,  (1)

averaged with equal weight over pairs (lower is better). Expected calibration error (ECE, ten equal-
width bins) is reported as a diagnostic [Guo et al., 2017]. Test benchmarks are disjoint from training
benchmarks; the evaluation runs offline with 8 hours per run.

3 Data

What we could and could not use. The official training data (measurement-db: item-level
responses of many subjects on curated benchmarks) require accepting terms with a Hugging Face

account. For most of the project we had no credentials and did not try to obtain the data in any other

way; the predictor and all its constants were fixed before any of it was seen. Its public file listing
(2026-10-22, revision 54074453da) held 281 benchmark directories: 14.8 GB of benchmarks,
subjects, items and responses tables plus 107.8 GB of traces and 154.7 GB of assets. On that
day the author's login became available and we downloaded the 163 competition-eligible directories
(binary, item-level responses; the eligibility flags live inside the benchmarks tables) without traces
or assets, and used them as described next. We also inspected the organisers' inventory of eligible

benchmarks (1,261 entries) but fitted nothing on any of them. The two local testbeds below only

evaluate design choices; the few constants they influenced are marked in Section 4 and probed in

Section 7.

The training release:  validation and a fit that was not shipped.

fit_prior_from_measurement_db.py builds one row per (subject, test condition, item)

with the mean response over trials as label--16,718 pairs with at least 20 distinct items from 144

benchmarks, 3,118,742 rows (pairs capped at 200 items; 16,303 pairs have the 80 items the protocol

requires)--and does two things (Appendix C). First, it evaluates the shipped prior-plus-shrinkage rule

with the protocol in closed form (50/50 split, 31 random acquisitions, Eq. 1; without the item-level

components, which change the ALC by < 0.001 locally): ALC 0.2051 [0.2042, 0.2060] against

0.2396 [0.2380, 0.2411] for the organisers' empirical mean and 0.2470 for the constant, with 0 = 4
the best of {2, 4, 8}. The ordering and the size of the gain measured locally thus transfer to real

systems on real benchmarks. Second, it fits an L2-penalised logistic regression on exactly the feature

terms of the hand-set prior plus a provider table and benchmark fixed effects (nuisance parameters;

the shipped intercept is their mean), pairs weighted equally, with five-fold cross-validation over

whole benchmarks. The fitted prior loses to the hand-set one on held-out benchmarks at every penalty

(budget-0 Brier 0.2421­0.2455 against 0.2388; paired differences at the selected setting +0.0033

[+0.0030, +0.0036] at budget 0 and +0.0009 [+0.0008, +0.0009] in ALC), and an intercept-only

prior is worse still (0.2519). The ship-only-if-better rule therefore kept the hand-set constants; no

coefficient in the submission derives from the release, and its only influence on the shipped predictor

is this decision. Where the data speak, the fitted signs agree with the hand-set values; what held-out

benchmarks punish is the provider table (Appendix C).

An item-text difficulty prior (pre-registered; not shipped). Benchmark difficulty dominates

the pair rate (benchmark means have standard deviation 0.223) and the attribute prior cannot see
it. We therefore tested, with design and ship rule fixed in PREREG.md (SHA-256 bb54af425d. . . )
before any fit, whether the item text predicts difficulty on unseen benchmarks: a ridge regression
on 262,144 signed-hashed buckets (words, bigrams, character trigrams, item_features) and 16
text statistics (length, LATEX, code, answer choices, . . . ), trained on the item logit the hand-set
prior leaves unexplained (109,233 items, benchmarks weighted equally) and stacked onto the prior
as T = W s + B with W, B fitted on out-of-fold scores; shrinkage is unchanged. On the same

benchmark-held-out folds, every choice nested inside the training benchmarks, the ALC changes by

-0.0000 [-0.0013, +0.0014] (cluster bootstrap over 135 benchmarks), closing -0.1%, 0.1% and 0.1%
of the gap to the pair-rate oracle at n = 0, 1, 3. The inner CV chose the largest penalty of the grid in
5 of 5 folds and the out-of-fold slope W was negative in all 5 (-0.32 to -0.03): text patterns learned

on some benchmarks point the wrong way on others. Exploratory variants registered afterwards
(penalties up to 104; statistics alone) give +0.0002 and +0.0001, the local testbeds +0.0019 (real,

not significant) and +0.0003 (synthetic, not significant). Headroom exists--knowing each held-out

benchmark's mean rate would gain -0.0061 ALC--but item text in this form does not reach it; the

rule fails and the submission is unchanged (Appendix D).

Real Ollama testbed. To obtain item-level correctness labels of real language models we ran

four small open-weight models locally with Ollama (Q4_K_M quantisation, greedy decoding, CPU

only): Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B, 3B and 7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Three public benchmarks
were used, none of which appears in the PAIEC inventory (only a derived variant, mr_gsm8k, does):
the first 200 GSM8K test items, the first 200 SVAMP test items (with the SVAMP problem type
as item_features), and a 300-item mix of ten BIG-Bench-Hard subtasks (30 items each, subtask

the final answer"; exact match after numeric or option normalisation) and, for GSM8K/SVAMP, an

Synthetic IRT testbed. Because the real testbed is small (small models, three benchmarks), we
complement it with a generator that follows a two-parameter item response model [Lord, 1980,
Embretson and Reise, 2000]. Subject s has ability s correlated with its visible attributes (release
year, reasoning effort, parameter count) plus noise; benchmark b has a location µb, a discrimination ab
and K = 5 topic clusters with difficulty offsets ck  N (0, t2opic); item i in cluster k(i) has difficulty
bi = µb + ck(i) +  zlen(i) + i and Pr(ysi = 1) = (ab(s - bi)). Item texts mix cluster-specific
vocabulary (35% of tokens), benchmark template words, ambient vocabulary and numbers, so that
lexical similarity carries difficulty information exactly when topic > 0; half of the benchmarks
expose a tier=easy/medium/hard feature derived from difficulty terciles. The main setting uses
10 subjects × 16 benchmarks = 160 pairs with 100­240 items each, topic = 0.70 and  = 0.3; a
sweep varies both. Because the true probabilities are known, we can report two oracle references: the
Brier score of the true item probabilities (the floor of any predictor) and that of the evaluation-set
success rate used as a constant (the floor of any predictor without item-level information).

4 Method

All constants below are hand-set and shipped in model.py; the few whose values were adjusted after
looking at the local testbeds are marked  and have sensitivity analyses in Section 7.

Budget-0 prior. A logistic prior p0 = (z) with z = clip(zsubj +zitem, ±1.5), so p0  [0.18, 0.82].
The subject part is zsubj = 0.7 · (0.35 clip(t - 2024.5, ±2) + e + 0.25 clip(log2(N/30B), ±4) +
0.1 [non-default harness]), where t is the release year (fractional), e  {0, 0.15, 0.3, 0.45} encodes
an empty, low, other or high reasoning_effort, and N is a parameter count parsed from the
name ("7b", "70B"); when no count is present, name tokens such as mini/nano/flash contribute -0.3
and pro/opus/ultra contribute 0.2. An intercept (0) and a per-provider table (0 entries) complete
the subject part; both are empty in the shipped predictor and are the slots a fitted coefficient table
would fill. The item part parses key=value pairs in item_features: a tier/difficulty/level value
matching hard/expert/. . . adds -0.6, easy/basic/. . . adds 0.5, a numeric level   [1, 5] adds
-0.25( - 3), and points above one subtract 0.1 each (capped at five); finally the item length adds
-0.12 clip(log2(L/600), ±3) for L characters. The directions encode common knowledge (newer,
larger, reasoning-enabled subjects do better; harder, longer, higher-value items are failed more often);
the magnitudes were chosen to move the prior by at most a few tenths of a logit per attribute and were
not tuned on either testbed.

Pair-level shrinkage. Given n acquired labels y1, . . . , yn of the same pair with item logits zi(tje)m
and mean z¯, the prior rate is r = (clip(zsubj + z¯)) and the posterior mean of a Beta­binomial model
with prior strength 0 = 4 pseudo-counts is  = (0r + j yj)/(0 + n) [Efron and Morris, 1975].
The pair estimate for a target item is ppair = (logit  + zitem - z¯), i.e. the item cues shift the pair
rate on the logit scale.

Item-level component 1: ridge features. From n  7 labels, three cheap features of the item
text--log length, digit density and log(1+number of sentences)--are standardised over the acquired
items and a logistic regression with offset logit ppair and ridge penalty  = 4 is fitted by six Newton
steps; its adjustment clip(f~, ±1) is added to the target's logit.

Item-level component 2: TF-IDF nearest neighbours. Item texts (content, features and interactors,
truncated to 4000 characters) are tokenised into lower-case words and numbers; the acquired set
defines the vocabulary and document frequencies, with sublinear term frequency, smoothed idf and
2 normalisation (bigram weight 1; target terms absent from the acquired vocabulary are dropped:

no). With cosine similarities sj between the target and acquired item j and weights wj = (sj)2, the
correction is a shrunken kernel-weighted residual,

p = clip a + n · j wj (yj - a) ,  N0 = 4,  = 2,  (2)
                 n + N0  + j wj

where the anchor a is the pair estimate (optionally including the ridge adjustment) and  = 2 by
default. With at most 31 short documents most bigrams are unique, so cosine similarities are small
(typically below 0.15 even for related items), which deliberately keeps the correction small unless
items share many terms; Appendix A compares a sharper unigram-only representation.

Leave-one-out gating. From n  5 labels, every configuration in {ridge off, on} × { 
{0, 1, 2, 4}} ( = 0 means no kNN) is scored by its leave-one-out Brier score on the acquired
labels themselves: label j is predicted from the other n - 1 labels, including a leave-one-out shrink-
age estimate and a leave-one-out ridge fit. The best configuration replaces plain shrinkage only if its
mean per-item improvement exceeds 2 standard error(s) of that mean; otherwise the predictor returns
ppair. This makes adaptation opt-in per pair and budget: a pair whose labels show no exploitable
structure is predicted by shrinkage alone. Below 5 labels no item-level component is used at all (in
preliminary runs an always-on kNN from one or three labels only added noise at B1 and B3).

Acquisition policy. labeling.py implements the streaming interface and never raises. Candidates
are accepted in stream order (uniformly random, hence the same label distribution as the evaluator's
default) except that a candidate whose maximum TF-IDF cosine similarity to an already acquired
item exceeds 0.6 is skipped while fewer than 90% of the remaining candidates are needed; it
always accepts when the remaining candidates are needed to spend the budget. We compare this
novelty filter with the evaluator's random policy, plain stream order, the organisers' uncertainty band
|p - 0.5|  0.15 [Truong et al., 2026], and a novelty-plus-uncertainty variant (skip |p - 0.5| > 0.45
once 7 labels are known).

5 Implementation

Predictions are clipped to [0.01, 0.99]; predict is wrapped in a fallback chain (full model  shrink-
age only  smoothed label mean  0.5), accepts labels given as booleans, numbers or strings, toler-
ates missing fields and non-dictionary inputs, and is deterministic. The submission ZIP (11.7 kB; mem-
bers README.md, labeling.py, model.py) contains no models.txt and no requirements.txt:
model.py imports only math and re, and labeling.py additionally importlib, os and sys to
load its sibling model.py by path; it loads no files and makes no network calls. The organisers'
check_submission_zip.py (commit cc88a959) passes on the ZIP; our smoke test calls predict
with the organisers' sample inputs at every budget, with malformed labels and inputs (42 cases) and
checks determinism. Mean latency is 1.92 ms per call on short items and 0.99 ms at budget 31 with
300-word items (maximum 27.0 ms), about 1,010 calls per second on one laptop core.

6 Experimental setup

Our harness reproduces the protocol of Section 2: items of each pair are split 50/50 with a seeded
shuffle, the acquisition pool is streamed in a seeded random order, the policy under test selects up
to 31 labels, and the nested prefixes of that one trajectory are passed to every predictor for every
evaluation item, so all methods are compared on identical labels. The evaluator's default policy
is reproduced as accept-with-probability labels_remaining/items_remaining. We report Brier ALC
with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over pairs (1,000 resamples), paired bootstrap differences
between methods (same pairs, same trajectories), per-budget Brier scores and pooled ECE. Baselines:
the constant 0.5; the organisers' empirical mean (their code: 0.5 without matching labels, the raw
mean otherwise); our prior alone; shrinkage alone; shrinkage with each item-level component always
on; the shipped LOO-gated model.

Main comparison. Table 1 and Figure 1 give the headline numbers. On the real testbed the
shipped predictor reaches 0.2224 [0.1949, 0.2454] against 0.2871 [0.2369, 0.3303] for the empirical
mean and 0.25 for the constant; the paired difference to the empirical mean is -0.0647 [-0.0872,
-0.0415] (significant; the full model is better on 82% of pairs) and to the constant -0.0276 [-0.0506,
-0.0050] (significant). On the synthetic testbed the corresponding ALCs are 0.1797 [0.1691, 0.1900]
versus 0.2208 and 0.25, with paired differences -0.0411 [-0.0488, -0.0339] and -0.0703 [-0.0805,
-0.0592] (both significant). The empirical mean is worse than the constant at n = 1 on both testbeds
(B1 = 0.330 and 0.498) and only overtakes it from n = 3 (synthetic) and n = 7 (real); on the real
testbed its ALC (0.2871) is above the constant's 0.25 (paired difference +0.0371 [-0.0081, +0.0779],
not significant).

Headroom. The oracle rows explain why. On the synthetic testbed, knowing the true probability of
every item gives an ALC of 0.1379, knowing only the evaluation-set success rate 0.1598: perfect
item-level discrimination is worth 0.0219 ALC, to be recovered from 15­31 labels at budgets that
carry 30% of the weight. Shrinkage (0.1797) is 0.0199 above the rate oracle; that gap is the price
of estimating a rate from few labels, concentrated at n  3 (B1: 0.041; B31: -0.003), and only a
better prior, i.e. training data, can reduce it. On the real testbed the gap is 0.0266 (0.2228 vs. 0.1961).
The two parts of the headroom are of similar size but call for different remedies: a prior fitted to real
subjects and benchmarks, and item difficulty information from outside the pair.

Prior strength and text signal. Figure 2 gives the ALC for 0  {1, 2, 4, 8} (shrinkage is best
at 2 on synthetic and 8 on real; full-model range 0.0038 and 0.0182; we ship 0 = 4, the smallest
worst-case regret and the best of {2, 4, 8} on the training release) and the synthetic sweep over topic,
where the item-level effect stays two orders of magnitude below the item-level headroom (0.0080 to
0.0306): the gate protects against harm, it cannot create information.

Acquisition policies. None of the five policies we compared is significantly different from the
evaluator's random policy on either testbed (Appendix A, Table 6): the shipped novelty filter
gives -0.0013 [-0.0038, +0.0013] (synthetic) and -0.0044 [-0.0108, +0.0025] (real), the organisers'
uncertainty band +0.0001 and -0.0041. Selecting by |p - 0.5| distorts the pair-rate estimate that
carries most of the metric; we ship the policy closest to random that removes near-duplicates.

Frontier-like subjects. The official test set has subjects our testbeds lack (2025­2026 releases, long
reasoning efforts, very large models, agent harnesses); over 160 such descriptions the budget-0 prior
ranges from 0.39 to 0.82 (the cap; Appendix E). What a wrong prior costs is exact under shrinkage
(Table 4): a prior of 0.7 on a pair whose true rate is 0.3 costs 0.062 ALC and loses to the constant
(excess 0.040), whereas shrinkage beats the constant whenever |r - 0.5|  0.2 and |p0 - r|  0.3. On
the real testbed an optimistic shift of every subject's prior logit costs significantly (+0.5: +0.0134), a
pessimistic one does not (-0.5: -0.0034, not significant); on the training release the hand-set prior
averages 0.510 against a mean pair rate of 0.537, so the intercept stays at zero.

Replication and calibration. Appendix B reports replication over generator seeds and acquisition
trajectories (the full model beats the empirical mean significantly in 4 of 4 further real trajectories).
Pooled ECE (Table 1) is 0.032/0.061 (synthetic/real) for the full model against 0.114/0.170 for
the empirical mean and 0.111/0.127 for the constant: shrinkage towards a sensible prior also fixes
calibration at small budgets.

8 Limitations

Local-versus-official gap. Our real testbed covers four small open models on three benchmarks
with two harnesses; the official test set contains frontier systems on many held-out benchmarks. In
particular the subject part of the prior (release date, reasoning effort, size) has essentially no variance
on our testbed and is validated only on the synthetic generator, whose attribute­ability relation we
chose ourselves. Absolute ALC values will differ; we expect the ordering shrinkage > empirical
mean > constant at small budgets to transfer because it follows from the metric, but the sign and size
of the item-level effects are testbed-dependent. A fitted prior did not help. On the training release
a logistic prior over the same visible attributes lost to the hand-set one under benchmark-held-out
cross-validation, and an intercept-only prior lost by more: across benchmarks the pair success rate is
dominated by benchmark difficulty, which the interface hides for test benchmarks, so visible subject
attributes buy little at budget 0 and the gain over the empirical mean comes from shrinkage. Richer
priors need information about the test benchmark itself. The most obvious source, the item text, did
not provide it in our pre-registered test (hashed n-grams and text statistics, Section 3); pretrained text
encoders remain untested, and labels of other subjects on the same benchmark are outside the stateless
interface. Small real testbed. 17 pairs give wide confidence intervals; non-significant differences are
stated as such. Stateless by design. Labels are never pooled across subjects of the same benchmark
(possible via benchmark_id, but outside the spirit of the task). Weak text signal. With at most 31
labels, lexical similarity recovers little of the item-level headroom on either testbed. Acquisition
filter without measurable effect. The novelty filter fires on templated real-testbed items (Appendix A)
but changes the ALC by less than 10-4 relative to stream order; its value on heavily templated
benchmarks is untested.

Gating. Replacing the LOO gate by always-on components at their default settings gives 0.1808

Sentence embeddings. The embeddings were computed offline for the real testbed (the model is
on the validator's allow-list via models.txt, so it could have been shipped). They change the full

to the acquired vocabulary separates same-topic from other-topic synthetic items far better (mean
cosine about 0.3 versus 0.1 in the generator) but makes the always-on kNN worse: paired against

+0.0006 [-0.0002, +0.0018]. With a handful of neighbours per topic the kernel-weighted residual is
dominated by label noise; the bigram-diluted similarity acts as a regulariser. Item-level signal exists
(the oracles show it) but 31 labels are too few to exploit it through similarity alone.

Acquisition policies. Table 6 compares policies for the full predictor on identical pools and streams.
Plain stream order differs from the evaluator's random policy by -0.0013 [-0.0038, +0.0013] (synthetic,
not significant) and -0.0044 [-0.0107, +0.0019] (real, not significant)--both are uniform samples,

closest to random while removing redundant items.

Table 3 gives the budget-0 prior p0 of the shipped constants for subject descriptions that the official

Discussion. The official test set has subjects our testbeds lack (2025­2026 releases, long reasoning
efforts, very large models, agent harnesses). Appendix E tabulates the budget-0 prior over 160 such
descriptions: p0 ranges from 0.39 to 0.82 (the cap); the organisers' GPT-4o and 2026 medium-effort
sample subjects receive 0.49 and 0.66, our testbed models 0.36­0.39. What a wrong prior costs is
exact: with 0 = 4 and n labels the expected Brier excess over the pair-rate oracle is E[(n - r)2]
with binomial successes (Table 4). A prior of 0.7 on a pair whose true rate is 0.3 costs 0.062 ALC
and loses to the constant (excess 0.040); shrinkage beats the constant whenever |r - 0.5|  0.2 and

training release the hand-set prior averages 0.510 against a mean pair rate of 0.537, i.e. it is not
optimistic for real systems. We keep the intercept at zero and name it as the first constant to change if
the official formative score points the other way.

                                            Abstract

          The Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC) asks for the probability that an
          AI system (the subject) answers a benchmark item correctly, given the subject's
           visible attributes, the item text, and between 0 and 31 labels acquired on the same
           subject­benchmark pair. Scoring is the Brier area-under-the-learning-curve (ALC),
          which places 60% of its weight on budgets of at most seven labels. We describe a
          submission built without the official training release (measurement-db is gated
           and we had no credentials), i.e. a predictor with no fitted parameters: a hand-set
           attribute prior at budget zero, beta-binomial shrinkage of the pair success rate
           towards that prior, and two item-level adaptation components--a ridge-logistic
           adjustment on cheap item features and a TF-IDF nearest-neighbour correction--that
           are switched on only when a leave-one-out test on the acquired labels shows a clear
           improvement. A streaming acquisition policy removes near-duplicate candidates.
           Because the test data are private, we reproduce the official protocol locally on (i) a
           real testbed of 15 subject­benchmark pairs generated with four small open models
          (Qwen2.5 1.5B/3B/7B, Llama 3.2 3B) on GSM8K, SVAMP and BIG-Bench-Hard,
           and (ii) a synthetic two-parameter IRT testbed with oracle bounds. The shipped
           predictor reaches a Brier ALC of 0.2183 (95% CI 0.1923­0.2410) on the real
           testbed against 0.2745 for the organisers' empirical-mean baseline and 0.25 for
           the constant predictor, and 0.1797 against 0.2208 on the synthetic testbed. Oracle
           analysis splits the remaining headroom into two comparable parts--rate estimation
           at small budgets, which only a better prior can reduce, and item-level discrimination,
          which 31 labels cannot deliver: the gated item-level components change the ALC
           by +0.0000 (synthetic) and -0.0004 (real) relative to shrinkage alone, sentence
           embeddings did not beat TF-IDF, and uncertainty sampling did not beat random
           acquisition. The predictor is pure standard-library Python, deterministic, and runs
           at about 499 calls per second on one CPU core; the submission ZIP passes the
           organisers' validator. Code and text were produced with the help of an LLM coding
           assistant (Claude).

1 Introduction

Evaluating a new AI system on the full set of available benchmarks is expensive, and the number
of benchmarks keeps growing [Liang et al., 2023]. A line of recent work therefore asks how well
performance can be predicted rather than measured: from a few hundred carefully chosen items [Polo
et al., 2024, Perlitz et al., 2024], from the scores of other models [Ruan et al., 2024], or from a handful
of labelled instances of the new model itself [Pacchiardi et al., 2024]. PAIEC [Stanford AIMS Lab,

Technical report, Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC), 40th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems
(NeurIPS 2026).
2026] turns this into a forecasting task with a precise interface: a function predict([subject,
item], labeled) must return Pr(correct) for one subject­item pair, where labeled contains the
labels already acquired on the same subject­benchmark pair at budgets n  {0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31}. Pairs
are scored by the Brier score [Brier, 1950] at each budget and aggregated into a Brier ALC with
weights (0.1, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.1)--the trapezoidal area under the Brier-versus-log2(n+1) curve.

Two facts about this metric shaped our design. First, 60% of the weight sits at n  7, where only the
pair-level success rate can be estimated with any precision; item-level discrimination can pay off only
at n = 15 and n = 31, which together carry 30%. Second, the organisers' empirical-mean baseline is
worse than the constant 0.5 at n = 1 (its prediction is 0 or 1), so the single most valuable ingredient
is a well-shrunk estimate of the pair rate. Our predictor therefore shrinks first: an attribute-based
prior at n = 0, a beta-binomial posterior mean of the pair rate for n  1 [Efron and Morris, 1975],
and only then item-level adaptation, which is gated by a leave-one-out (LOO) test on the acquired
labels so that it is applied only when the pair's own labels show that it helps.

We also faced a constraint that defines the submission: the official training release
(aims-foundations/measurement-db) is gated behind a Hugging Face login, and the machine
used for this work had no credentials; we deliberately did not create any account. The predictor
therefore contains no parameter fitted on any response data. Every constant is a documented prior
(Section 4), and the report tests their sensitivity. We see this as a strong, fully reproducible baseline
for the competition and as a measurement of how far test-time adaptation alone can go.

Contributions. (1) A standard-library-only predictor and streaming acquisition policy that pass the
official validator and run at hundreds of calls per second. (2) A local reproduction of the evaluation
protocol--pairs, 50/50 acquisition/evaluation pools, a fixed random stream, nested budgets, Brier
ALC and ECE--on a real testbed of 15 pairs built with local open models and on a synthetic IRT
testbed with known item probabilities. (3) An analysis with paired bootstrap confidence intervals
of what helps (prior, shrinkage), what helps marginally (item-level components), and what does not
(sentence embeddings over TF-IDF, uncertainty sampling over random acquisition), together with
oracle bounds that locate the remaining headroom.

2 Task, interface and metric

A subject is a dictionary of strings (normalized_name, provider, release_date, access_date,
harness, harness_version, reasoning_effort, subject_features_extra); an item has
item_content (the task text), item_features (e.g. tier=medium;points=1), interactors,
and an anonymous benchmark_id shared by all items of a benchmark. Missing values are empty

strings. For every subject­benchmark pair with at least 80 distinct items, the evaluator splits

the items 50/50 into an acquisition pool and an evaluation pool; acquisition items are never

scored. Candidates from the acquisition pool arrive in a fixed random order and an optional
acquisition_function(input, prediction, labeled, context) returns True to reveal a

label (31 at most); without it the evaluator samples labels uniformly. The nested label sets after
0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 reveals are passed to predict for every evaluation item, giving Brier scores
B0, . . . , B31 and

ALC = 0.1 B0 + 0.2 B1 + 0.2 B3 + 0.2 B7 + 0.2 B15 + 0.1 B31,  (1)

averaged with equal weight over pairs (lower is better). Expected calibration error (ECE, ten equal-
width bins) is reported as a diagnostic [Guo et al., 2017]. Test benchmarks are disjoint from the
training benchmarks. The evaluation runs offline (no package installation, no model downloads at
run time, 8 hours per run).

3 Data

What we could and could not use. The official training data (measurement-db: item-level
responses of many subjects on curated benchmarks) require accepting terms with a Hugging Face
account; without credentials we could not download them, and we did not try to obtain them in any
other way. We also inspected the organisers' inventory of eligible benchmarks (1,261 entries with
slug, authors, code and data URLs and venue, mostly NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, ICLR and
ICML papers from 2024­2026) but did not fit anything on any of them. Consequently, no training

data of any kind entered the shipped predictor. The two local testbeds below were used only to

were influenced by them are identified in Section 4 and probed by sensitivity analyses in Section 7.

Real Ollama testbed. To obtain item-level correctness labels of real language models we ran
four small open-weight models locally with Ollama (Q4_K_M quantisation, greedy decoding, CPU
only): Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B, 3B and 7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Three public benchmarks
were used, none of which appears in the PAIEC inventory (only a derived variant, mr_gsm8k, does):
the first 200 GSM8K test items, the first 200 SVAMP test items (with the SVAMP problem type
as item_features), and a 300-item mix of ten BIG-Bench-Hard subtasks (30 items each, subtask
name as item_features). The 7B model was run on SVAMP only and its run was cut short at 126
items because the shared Ollama server was saturated by other jobs; a pair needs 80 items, so it is
kept. Two harnesses are represented: a direct-answer prompt ("reply with only the final answer";
exact match after numeric or option normalisation; the three smaller models on all three benchmarks)
and, for GSM8K/SVAMP, an earlier 8-shot chain-of-thought run of the Qwen models whose greedy
answers we re-used. Each (model, harness, benchmark) triple with at least 80 items is one pair, giving
15 pairs, 3,226 labelled items, and success rates from 8.0% to 79.0% (Table 3 in the appendix). The
subject dictionaries carry the real model names, providers, release dates and harness names so that
the budget-0 prior is exercised on realistic inputs. Generation took about two hours of wall-clock
time on a shared CPU; the raw records (prompt, raw answer, normalised answer, label) are released
with the code.

Synthetic IRT testbed. Because the real testbed is small (small models, three benchmarks), we
complement it with a generator that follows a two-parameter item response model [Lord, 1980,
Embretson and Reise, 2000]. Subject s has ability s correlated with its visible attributes (release
year, reasoning effort, parameter count) plus noise; benchmark b has a location µb, a discrimination ab
and K = 5 topic clusters with difficulty offsets ck  N (0, t2opic); item i in cluster k(i) has difficulty
bi = µb + ck(i) +  zlen(i) + i and Pr(ysi = 1) = (ab(s - bi)). Item texts mix cluster-specific
vocabulary (35% of tokens), benchmark template words, ambient vocabulary and numbers, so that
lexical similarity carries difficulty information exactly when topic > 0; half of the benchmarks
expose a tier=easy/medium/hard feature derived from difficulty terciles. The main setting uses
10 subjects × 16 benchmarks = 160 pairs with 100­240 items each, topic = 0.70 and  = 0.3; a
sweep varies both. Because the true probabilities are known, we can report two oracle references: the
Brier score of the true item probabilities (the floor of any predictor) and that of the evaluation-set
success rate used as a constant (the floor of any predictor without item-level information).

4 Method

All constants below are hand-set and shipped in model.py; the few whose values were adjusted after
looking at the local testbeds are marked  and have sensitivity analyses in Section 7.

Budget-0 prior. A logistic prior p0 = (z) with z = clip(zsubj +zitem, ±1.5), so p0  [0.18, 0.82].
The subject part is zsubj = 0.7 · (0.35 clip(t - 2024.5, ±2) + e + 0.25 clip(log2(N/30B), ±4) +
0.1 [non-default harness]), where t is the release year (fractional), e  {0, 0.15, 0.3, 0.45} encodes
an empty, low, other or high reasoning_effort, and N is a parameter count parsed from the
name ("7b", "70B"); when no count is present, name tokens such as mini/nano/flash contribute -0.3
and pro/opus/ultra contribute 0.2. The item part parses key=value pairs in item_features: a
tier/difficulty/level value matching hard/expert/. . . adds -0.6, easy/basic/. . . adds 0.5, a numeric
level   [1, 5] adds -0.25( - 3), and points above one subtract 0.1 each (capped at five); finally
the item length adds -0.12 clip(log2(L/600), ±3) for L characters. The directions encode common
knowledge (newer, larger, reasoning-enabled subjects do better; harder, longer, higher-value items
are failed more often); the magnitudes were chosen to move the prior by at most a few tenths of a
logit per attribute and were not tuned on either testbed.

Pair-level shrinkage. Given n acquired labels y1, . . . , yn of the same pair with item logits zi(tje)m
and mean z¯, the prior rate is r = (clip(zsubj + z¯)) and the posterior mean of a Beta­binomial model
with prior strength 0 = 4 pseudo-counts is  = (0r + j yj)/(0 + n) [Efron and Morris, 1975].

                                                           3
The pair estimate for a target item is ppair = (logit  + zitem - z¯), i.e. the item cues shift the pair
rate on the logit scale.

Item-level component 1: ridge features. From n  7 labels, three cheap features of the item

text--log length, digit density and log(1+number of sentences)--are standardised over the acquired
items and a logistic regression with offset logit ppair and ridge penalty  = 4 is fitted by six Newton
steps; its adjustment clip(f~, ±1) is added to the target's logit.

Item-level component 2: TF-IDF nearest neighbours. Item texts (content, features and interactors,
truncated to 4000 characters) are tokenised into lower-case words and numbers; the acquired set
defines the vocabulary and document frequencies, with sublinear term frequency, smoothed idf and
2 normalisation (bigram weight 1; target terms absent from the acquired vocabulary are dropped:
no). With cosine similarities sj between the target and acquired item j and weights wj = (sj)2, the
correction is a shrunken kernel-weighted residual,

p = clip a + n · j wj (yj - a) ,  N0 = 4,  = 2,  (2)
                 n + N0  + j wj

where the anchor a is the pair estimate (optionally including the ridge adjustment). The default
scale is  = 2. With at most 31 short documents most bigrams are unique, so the bigram terms
dominate the vector norms and cosine similarities are small (typically below 0.15 even for lexically
related items); this deliberately keeps the correction small unless items share many terms. Section 7
compares this with a sharper unigram-only representation.

Leave-one-out gating. From n  5 labels, every configuration in {ridge off, on} × { 
{0, 1, 2, 4}} ( = 0 means no kNN) is scored by its leave-one-out Brier score on the acquired
labels themselves: label j is predicted from the other n - 1 labels, including a leave-one-out shrink-
age estimate and a leave-one-out ridge fit. The best configuration replaces plain shrinkage only if its
mean per-item improvement exceeds 2 standard error(s) of that mean; otherwise the predictor returns
ppair. This makes adaptation opt-in per pair and budget: a pair whose labels show no exploitable
structure is predicted by shrinkage alone. Below 5 labels no item-level component is used at all (in
preliminary runs an always-on kNN correction from one or three labels only added noise at B1 and
B3, the budgets that carry most of the metric).

Acquisition policy. labeling.py implements the streaming interface and never raises. Candidates
are accepted in stream order (which, being uniformly random, yields the same distribution of labels
as the evaluator's default) except that a candidate whose maximum TF-IDF cosine similarity to an
already acquired item exceeds 0.6 is skipped while the remaining budget is not urgent (fewer than 90%
of the remaining candidates needed); it always accepts when the remaining candidates are needed
to spend the budget and returns False once the budget is exhausted. We compare this novelty filter
with the evaluator's random policy, with plain stream order, with the organisers' uncertainty band
|p - 0.5|  0.15 [Truong et al., 2026], and with a novelty-plus-uncertainty variant (skip candidates
with |p - 0.5| > 0.45 once 7 labels are known).

Calibration and robustness. Predictions are clipped to [0.01, 0.99]. predict is wrapped in a
fallback chain (full model  shrinkage only  smoothed label mean  0.5), accepts labels given as
booleans, numbers or strings, tolerates missing fields and non-dictionary inputs, and is deterministic.

6 Experimental setup

Our harness reproduces the protocol of Section 2: items of each pair are split 50/50 with a seeded
shuffle, the acquisition pool is streamed in a seeded random order, the policy under test selects up
to 31 labels, and the nested prefixes of that one trajectory are passed to every predictor for every
evaluation item, so all methods are compared on identical labels. The evaluator's default policy
is reproduced as accept-with-probability labels_remaining/items_remaining. We report Brier ALC
with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals over pairs (1,000 resamples), paired bootstrap differences
between methods (same pairs, same trajectories), per-budget Brier scores and pooled ECE. Baselines:
the constant 0.5; the organisers' empirical mean (their code, which returns 0.5 without matching
labels and the raw mean otherwise); our prior alone; shrinkage alone; shrinkage with each item-level
component always on; and the shipped LOO-gated model. Everything runs on CPU; the full suite
takes about an hour with four worker processes.

7 Results

Main comparison. Table 1 and Figure 1 give the headline numbers. On the real testbed the
shipped predictor reaches 0.2183 [0.1923, 0.2410] against 0.2745 [0.2302, 0.3117] for the empirical
mean and 0.25 for the constant; the paired difference to the empirical mean is -0.0562 [-0.0771,
-0.0351] (significant; the full model is better on 80% of pairs) and to the constant -0.0317 [-0.0574,
-0.0104] (significant). On the synthetic testbed the corresponding ALCs are 0.1797 [0.1691, 0.1900]
versus 0.2208 and 0.25, with paired differences -0.0411 [-0.0488, -0.0339] and -0.0703 [-0.0805,
-0.0592] (both significant). The empirical mean is worse than the constant at n = 1 on both testbeds
(B1 = 0.330 and 0.468) and only overtakes it from n = 3; on the real testbed its ALC (0.2745) is
above the constant's 0.25 (paired difference +0.0245 [-0.0200, +0.0608], not significant), confirming
the organisers' warning that an unsmoothed group mean can lose to 0.5.

Headroom. The oracle rows explain why. On the synthetic testbed, knowing the true probability of
every item gives an ALC of 0.1379, while knowing only the evaluation-set success rate gives 0.1598:
perfect item-level discrimination is worth 0.0219 ALC, and it would have to be recovered from 15­31
labels at the two budgets that carry 30% of the weight. Shrinkage (0.1797) is 0.0199 above the rate
oracle; that gap is the price of estimating a rate from few labels, concentrated at n  3 (B1: 0.041
above the oracle; B31: -0.003), and it can only be reduced by a better prior, i.e. by training data.
On the real testbed the rate oracle is 0.1966 against 0.2187 for shrinkage (gap 0.0221; B1: 0.045,
B31: 0.004). The two parts of the headroom are of similar size, but they call for different remedies:
the rate part needs a prior fitted to real subjects and benchmarks; the item part needs item difficulty
information from outside the pair.

Prior strength. Figure 2(left) and the appendix give the ALC for 0  {1, 2, 4, 8}. The best value
differs between testbeds (shrinkage: 2 on synthetic, 8 on real), reflecting the wider spread of pair
rates in the synthetic generator; the ALC of the full model varies by 0.0038 (synthetic) and 0.0135
(real) over the range, so the choice matters at the third decimal. We ship 0 = 4, the value with the
smallest worst-case regret over the two testbeds, and flag it as the constant most worth revisiting with
access to real training data: 0 should match the spread of success rates across the pairs actually
evaluated.

When does text help? The synthetic sweep (Figure 2, right; Table 5) varies how much difficulty is
explained by topic. With no topic or length signal (topic = 0.00,  = 0.0) the full model's paired
difference to shrinkage is +0.0002 [-0.0000, +0.0005]--the gate's cost when there is nothing to
find; at the strongest setting (topic = 1.00) it is -0.0002 [-0.0005, +0.0001], while the item-level
headroom (rate oracle minus true-probability oracle) grows from 0.0080 to 0.0306. The sign of the
item-level effect does turn with the signal strength, but its size stays two orders of magnitude below
the headroom: the gate protects against harm, it cannot create information.

sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings (computed offline for the real testbed;
the model is on the validator's allow-list via models.txt, so it could have been shipped) changes
the full model's ALC from 0.2183 to 0.2181 (paired -0.0002 [-0.0005, +0.0000], not significant)
and the always-on kNN variant from 0.2189 to 0.2206 (+0.0018 [-0.0008, +0.0047], not significant).

not significant) and -0.0009 [-0.0063, +0.0044] (real, not significant)--both are uniform samples,
so this is the trajectory-to-trajectory noise floor of the comparison. The shipped novelty filter gives
-0.0013 [-0.0038, +0.0013] and -0.0011 [-0.0067, +0.0048]; neither testbed contains near-duplicate
items at the 0.6 cosine threshold, so the filter never fires and the policy coincides with stream order (its
value, if any, is on benchmarks with templated or repeated items). The organisers' uncertainty band
gives +0.0001 [-0.0027, +0.0030] and -0.0027 [-0.0086, +0.0026], and the novelty-plus-uncertainty
variant -0.0013 and -0.0011: with a predictor whose predictions are nearly constant within a pair
at small budgets, selecting by |p - 0.5| mostly reorders a random sample, and once item-level
corrections exist it biases the sample towards items of intermediate predicted difficulty, which distorts
the pair-rate estimate that carries most of the metric. None of the policies is significantly different
from random on either testbed; we ship the one that is closest to random while removing redundant
items.

Replication and calibration. Regenerating the synthetic testbed with two further seeds gives full-
model ALCs of 0.2266 and 0.1963 (empirical mean 0.2737 and 0.2438; shrinkage 0.2264 and 0.1963).
On the real testbed, four further acquisition trajectories give full-model ALCs between 0.2102 and
0.2208 (empirical mean 0.2469­0.2851), and the full model beats the empirical mean significantly in
4 of 4 trajectories. A 3×3 grid over the kNN scale and anchor (  {1, 2, 4},   {1, 2, 4}) spans
0.1806­0.1809 on a smaller synthetic generator, i.e. the kernel constants matter little. Pooled ECE
(Table 1) is 0.032 (synthetic) and 0.046 (real) for the full model against 0.114 and 0.152 for the
empirical mean and 0.111/0.135 for the constant; shrinkage towards a sensible prior is also what fixes
calibration at small budgets.

8 Limitations

Local-versus-official gap. Our real testbed covers four small open models on three benchmarks
with two harnesses; the official test set contains frontier systems on many held-out benchmarks. In
particular the subject part of the prior (release date, reasoning effort, size) has essentially no variance
on our testbed and is validated only on the synthetic generator, whose attribute­ability relation we
chose ourselves. Absolute ALC values will differ; we expect the ordering shrinkage > empirical
mean > constant at small budgets to transfer because it follows from the metric, but the sign and
size of the item-level effects are testbed-dependent. No training data. With measurement-db the
prior and 0 could be fitted rather than set by hand; this is the obvious next step and would mostly
affect B0 and B1. Small real testbed. 15 pairs give wide confidence intervals; several differences
we report are not significant and are stated as such. Stateless by design. The predictor uses only the
labels passed to the current call; it does not pool labels across subjects on the same benchmark, which
the interface would in principle allow through benchmark_id but which we considered outside the
spirit of the task and fragile under concurrent evaluation. Weak text signal. With at most 31 labels,
lexical similarity recovers little of the available item-level headroom on either testbed; richer item
models would need data beyond the pair. Untested acquisition filter. The novelty filter never fired
on either testbed, so its effect on benchmarks with templated or repeated items is untested; on our
testbeds it is exactly stream order.

A Additional ablations

Gating. Replacing the LOO gate by always-on components at their default settings gives 0.1808
versus 0.1797 (synthetic; paired gated-always -0.0011 [-0.0017, -0.0005], significant) and 0.2169
versus 0.2183 (real; +0.0014 [-0.0019, +0.0045], not significant). The gate pays where always-on
components overfit (the synthetic ridge) and costs a little where a component carries real signal (on
the real testbed longer GSM8K problems are failed more often, which the always-on ridge exploits at
n  15); the second effect is within noise. Requiring 0, 1 or 2 standard errors of LOO improvement
gives full-model ALCs of 0.1808, 0.1799 and 0.1797 (synthetic) and 0.2178, 0.2182 and 0.2183
(real); we ship the most conservative setting.

Sharper lexical similarity does not help. A unigram-only TF-IDF whose target terms are restricted
to the acquired vocabulary separates same-topic from other-topic synthetic items far better (mean
cosine about 0.3 versus 0.1 in the generator) but makes the always-on kNN worse: paired against
the shipped representation, the always-on kNN changes by +0.0009 [-0.0001, +0.0020] (synthetic)
and +0.0017 [+0.0001, +0.0031] (real), and the gated full model by +0.0004 [+0.0001, +0.0008] and
+0.0001 [-0.0001, +0.0003]. With a handful of neighbours per topic the kernel-weighted residual is
dominated by label noise; the bigram-diluted similarity acts as a regulariser. Item-level signal exists
(the oracles show it) but 31 labels are too few to exploit it through similarity alone.

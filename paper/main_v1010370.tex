\begin{abstract}
The Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC) asks for the probability that an AI system (the \emph{subject}) answers a benchmark \emph{item} correctly, given visible attributes, the item text, and labels acquired at budgets of 0--31 per subject--benchmark pair; the score is a Brier area under the learning curve (ALC). The official streaming client passes \emph{one global list} with the labels acquired for \emph{all} sampled pairs to every prediction, whereas our earlier submission used only the target pair's labels. We now fit a random-effects logistic model to that list once per budget checkpoint: a hand-set attribute prior as offset, benchmark, subject and subject--benchmark effects, and an exact-item effect learned from other subjects' labels on the same item, with probit integration of the posterior uncertainty. Its prior standard deviations were chosen by nested, benchmark-held-out simulation of the official protocol on the official training release (\XpFrNPairs{} pairs, \XpFrNBench{} benchmarks), with design and ship rule pre-registered. In formative-sized test sets (at most 1,000 subject--item pairs) the ALC falls from \XpCASZeroAlc{} (our earlier predictor, as the evaluator would run it) to \XpCAXpAlc, a paired difference of \XpCADiffSZeroMean{} [\XpCADiffSZeroLo, \XpCADiffSZeroHi] (cluster bootstrap over \XpCANBench{} benchmarks); it is \XpCEDiffSZeroMean{} when cross-pair evidence only comes from different models and \XpCBDiffSZeroMean{} when all pairs of a benchmark are scored together. Benchmark difficulty and exact-item difficulty contribute about equally. On a 17-pair testbed of four small open models the difference is \XpRealDiffGlobalMean{} [\XpRealDiffGlobalLo, \XpRealDiffGlobalHi] for the pre-registered acquisition trajectory (not significant) and significant in \XpRealDiffGlobalNSigNeg{} of \XpRealNSeeds{} trajectories. An acquisition rule that skips items already labelled for another subject changes the ALC by a further \XpAcqMean{} [\XpAcqLo, \XpAcqHi]. The predictor is standard-library Python; one fit of a 62,000-label list takes \XpRtTwoThousandFit{} s and a prediction \XpRtTwoThousandMs{} ms. Earlier negative results (a fitted attribute prior, a pre-registered item-text prior) are summarised. Code and text were produced with an LLM coding assistant (Claude).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Evaluating every new AI system on every benchmark is expensive, and the number of benchmarks keeps growing~\citep{liang2022helm}. Recent work therefore predicts performance instead of measuring it: from a few hundred informative items selected with item response theory (IRT)~\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks,kipnis2025metabench,perlitz2024efficient}, adaptively per model~\citep{hofmann2025fluid,zhuang2025position}, from other models' scores~\citep{ruan2024observational,ye2023predictable}, or from a few labelled instances of the new model~\citep{pacchiardi2024hundred}. PAIEC~\citep{paiec2026} turns this into a forecasting task with a precise interface: \texttt{predict([subject, item], labeled)} returns $\Pr(\text{correct})$, and \texttt{labeled} holds the labels acquired so far, at budgets of $n\in\{0,1,3,7,15,31\}$ labels per subject--benchmark pair.

Our submission for rounds 1--3 read \texttt{labeled} as the target pair's own labels: a hand-set attribute prior, beta-binomial shrinkage of the pair rate~\citep{efron1975}, and item-level corrections gated by a leave-one-out test. It beat the organisers' empirical mean on two local testbeds and on the training release, while two attempts to improve the budget-0 prior with the release---a fitted attribute prior and a pre-registered item-text prior---failed under benchmark-held-out cross-validation. The diagnosis was that the residual error is dominated by \emph{benchmark difficulty}, which nothing visible at budget 0 reveals. Reading the official streaming client closely (Section~\ref{sec:task}) shows that the information is there from budget 1 on: \texttt{labeled} is one \emph{global} list with the labels of all sampled pairs. Other subjects' labels on the same benchmark reveal its difficulty, a subject's labels on other benchmarks its ability, and because acquisition and evaluation pools are split per pair, the very item being predicted is often labelled for another subject. The rules allow exactly this (``the submitted program may use the attributes and acquired labels supplied through the evaluation interface to adapt during evaluation'').

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1)~A precise statement of the evaluator's global-evidence protocol and a faithful multi-pair simulator of it on the official training release, with benchmark-held-out folds and five test-set configurations. (2)~A random-effects (Rasch-style) predictor fitted to the global list once per checkpoint~\citep{lord1980,breslow1993}, with an exact-item effect and probit integration~\citep{mackay1992}; pre-registered selection and ship rule; paired, cluster-bootstrapped gains at every budget; component ablations; a check that the shipped files reproduce the simulator inside the organisers' own client. (3)~A coverage-oriented acquisition rule. (4)~The earlier per-pair predictor, kept as fallback and for budget 0, and its negative results (Appendices~\ref{app:perpair}--\ref{app:oldresults}).

\paragraph{Related work and design choices.} Item response models---a subject ability and an item difficulty on a logit scale---are widely used for LLM benchmark data, to rank systems, find informative items and estimate full-benchmark scores from few items~\citep{rodriguez2021evaluation,polo2024tinybenchmarks,kipnis2025metabench}. These methods fit item parameters offline on hundreds of models; in PAIEC the test benchmarks are new, so difficulty must be learned \emph{during} evaluation from a few labels of a few subjects. We therefore keep the Rasch structure but replace free parameters by random effects with Gaussian priors (a generalised linear mixed model~\citep{breslow1993}), which shrinks a benchmark's difficulty towards the prior when it has one or three labels and lets it speak when it has hundreds, and we use one difficulty per item only where the item itself was labelled. Adaptive testing~\citep{hofmann2025fluid,zhuang2025position} chooses the next item from the current ability estimate; the PAIEC stream only lets a policy accept or skip a random candidate, and uncertainty-based skipping did not help in round 1 (Appendix~\ref{app:ablations}), so our acquisition rule only maximises coverage of distinct items. Work that predicts a model's scores from other models' scores~\citep{ruan2024observational,ye2023predictable} motivates pooling across subjects; our model does this at the item level and inside one evaluation run.

\section{Task, interface and the global label list}
\label{sec:task}

A \emph{subject} is a dictionary of strings (\texttt{normalized\_name}, \texttt{provider}, \texttt{release\_date}, \texttt{access\_date}, \texttt{harness}, \texttt{harness\_version}, \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, \texttt{subject\_features\_extra}); an \emph{item} has \texttt{item\_content}, \texttt{item\_features}, \texttt{interactors} and an anonymous \texttt{benchmark\_id}. Each subject--benchmark pair with at least 80 distinct items is split 50/50 into an acquisition and an evaluation pool, fixed across submissions. Acquisition candidates of a pair arrive in a fixed random order; an optional \texttt{acquisition\_function} returns \texttt{True} to reveal a label (31 per pair at most), otherwise labels are drawn uniformly. After 0, 1, 3, 7, 15 and 31 labels per pair every evaluation item is predicted, giving Brier scores~\citep{brier1950} $B_0,\dots,B_{31}$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ALC} = 0.1\,B_0 + 0.2\,B_1 + 0.2\,B_3 + 0.2\,B_7 + 0.2\,B_{15} + 0.1\,B_{31},
\label{eq:alc}
\end{equation}
averaged over pairs (lower is better). A formative evaluation samples at most 1,000 subject--item pairs (acquisition and evaluation pools together) from benchmarks of the private test pool; runs have 8 hours.

\paragraph{The global label list.} In the organisers' client (\texttt{tools/streaming\_ingestion.py}, \texttt{run\_streaming}) every coordinator event carries one list, \texttt{labeled = event["labeled"]}; at an \texttt{evaluate} event all distinct inputs of the checkpoint are predicted with \texttt{prediction\_stream(model\_path, unique, labeled, \dots)}, i.e.\ with the \emph{same} list, and the competition page states that \texttt{labeled} ``contains [[subject, item], label] entries across sampled subject--benchmark pairs''. At budget $n$ the list therefore holds up to $n$ labels of \emph{every} pair; at budget 0 it is empty. Our rounds 1--3 predictor, like the organisers' empirical-mean baseline, used only the target pair's labels; it selected them by benchmark and \emph{model name}, so as deployed it also pooled the labels of other subjects with the same name (other reasoning efforts, harnesses or test conditions). Using the other entries is permitted; what is forbidden is extracting test data or trying to identify the anonymous subjects and benchmarks, and we do neither: the model only uses anonymous keys (the subject's full attribute dictionary, \texttt{benchmark\_id}, item text) inside one evaluation run and keeps nothing across runs.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Budget-0 prior (unchanged).} $o(s,i)=\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}(s)+z_{\mathrm{item}}(i),\pm\CPriorCap)$, a hand-set logistic form: release year, reasoning effort, parameter count or size words in the name, and harness for the subject; tier/level/points cues in \texttt{item\_features} and item length for the item (Appendix~\ref{app:perpair}). At budget 0 the prediction is $\sigma(o)$.

\paragraph{Cross-pair random-effects model.} For a label $y_e$ of subject $s$ on item $i$ of benchmark $b$ in the global list, stage 1 is the logistic random-effects model
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{logit}\,\Pr(y_e=1) = o(s,i) + \mu + v_b + u_s + w_{sb},
\label{eq:xp}
\end{equation}
with independent priors $\mu\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_\mu^2)$, $v_b\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_b^2)$, $u_s\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_s^2)$, $w_{sb}\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_{sb}^2)$, subjects identified by their complete attribute dictionary (as the organisers' baseline does) and benchmarks by \texttt{benchmark\_id}. We compute the MAP estimate by backfitting: one Newton step per effect type and sweep, $\theta_g \leftarrow \theta_g + \big(\sum_{e\in g}(y_e-p_e)-\theta_g/\tau^2\big)/\big(\sum_{e\in g}p_e(1-p_e)+1/\tau^2\big)$, at most \XpSweeps{} sweeps (penalised quasi-likelihood for a GLMM~\citep{breslow1993}). The target's latent logit $m=o+\hat\mu+\hat v_b+\hat u_s+\hat w_{sb}$ (zero for groups without labels) has approximate posterior variance $V=\sum_g 1/(H_g+\tau_g^{-2})$, with $\tau_g^2$ for an unseen group, and $p=\sigma\big(m/\sqrt{1+\pi V/8}\big)$~\citep{mackay1992}. \emph{Exact-item effect (stage 2).} If the target item (same \texttt{benchmark\_id} and text) was labelled for other subjects, an item effect $d\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_d^2)$ is estimated from those labels by \XpItemNewton{} Newton steps on the conditional scale (their stage-1 logits multiplied by $c=\sqrt{1+\pi\tau_d^2/8}$, since stage 1 averages over item difficulty), and $p=\sigma\big((c\,m'+\hat d)/\sqrt{1+\pi\,\mathrm{Var}(d)/8}\big)$ with $m'$ the probit-scaled stage-1 logit; without matches this reduces to the stage-1 prediction. The model also has slots for a model-name effect and a name--benchmark effect, which the selection switched off. Shipped prior standard deviations: $\tau_\mu=\XpTauMu$, $\tau_b=\XpTauBench$, $\tau_s=\XpTauSubj$, $\tau_{sb}=\XpTauPair$, $\tau_d=\XpTauItem$ (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\paragraph{Engineering.} \texttt{labeled} is identical for all calls of a checkpoint, so the parsed list and the fit are cached at module level under a fingerprint (length and a hash of about 70 evenly spaced entries) and replaced atomically under a lock; separate worker processes fit once each. A list of \XpRtTwoThousandLabels{} labels is fitted in \XpRtTwoThousandFit{} s; a cache-hit call takes \XpRtTwoThousandMs{} ms (Table~\ref{tab:xpruntime}). The code is standard-library Python, deterministic, and falls back to the rounds 1--3 predictor on any failure.

\paragraph{Fallback: the per-pair predictor of rounds 1--3.} Beta-binomial shrinkage of the pair rate towards the prior ($\alpha_0=\CAlpha$ pseudo-counts) and, from \CLooMinN{} labels, a TF-IDF nearest-neighbour correction and (from \CRidgeMinN{} labels) a ridge adjustment on item features, each switched on only when a leave-one-out test on the pair's labels shows an improvement of \CLooSeMult{} standard errors (Appendix~\ref{app:perpair}). It handles budget 0 and any failure of the cross-pair model.

\paragraph{Acquisition: fresh first.} With cross-pair learning, a label on an item nobody has labelled can help every other subject whose evaluation pool contains that item, whereas a second label on an already labelled item adds less. \texttt{labeling.py} therefore accepts candidates in stream order (a uniformly random sample, as the default policy) except that it skips a candidate whose item is already in the global list for another subject while $\text{labels\_remaining}<\text{items\_remaining}$, so the budget is always spent. The function has no \texttt{prediction} parameter, so the client does not call \texttt{predict} for every candidate.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Frame.} The official training release (\texttt{aims-foundations/measurement-db}, revision \texttt{\FitRevisionShort}) gives \XpFrNPairs{} pairs of (subject, test condition) on \XpFrNBench{} benchmarks with at least 20 items (\XpFrNPairsEighty{} with 80), \XpFrNRows{} item-level labels (mean over trials) and \XpFrNItems{} distinct items. Each pair keeps its \XpFrMaxItems{} answered items with the lowest benchmark-level hash rank, so subjects of a benchmark share items as far as their answered sets allow. The release is dominated by test-condition variants of one model: \XpFrShareNameBench\% of pairs share (model name, benchmark) with another pair.

\paragraph{Simulator.} The five benchmark folds of round 2 hold out whole benchmarks. A \emph{test set} is a set of pairs of one fold that shares one global list. Per pair (fixed across test sets) items are split 50/50 and the acquisition pool is permuted at random; budget $n$ is its first $n$ labels (the official random policy yields a uniformly random subset in stream order); at budget $n$ every prediction of the test set receives the union of all its pairs' first $n$ labels. Configurations: \textbf{A} (primary, formative-like): 1--3 random benchmarks, 10 random pairs with at least 80 items split over them, 100 items per pair ($\le$1,000 subject--item pairs), 300 test sets per fold; \textbf{E}: as A but no two pairs share a model name, so cross-pair evidence comes only from different models; \textbf{B}: all pairs of one benchmark (up to 300, 200 items each); \textbf{C}: up to 2,000 pairs of a whole fold in one list; \textbf{D}: A plus up to two pairs with 20--79 items that are evaluated on all items and never acquired (the page does not say how such pairs are handled). Per-pair ALC; means over (test set, pair) units; 95\% CIs by a cluster bootstrap over benchmarks (2,000 resamples).

\paragraph{Pre-registration and selection.} \texttt{experiments/results/round4/PREREG.md} (SHA-256 prefix \texttt{\XpPreregSha}, \XpPreregConsistent{} with every result file) fixed configurations A--D, predictors, the grid of \XpGridN{} settings ($\tau_b$, $\tau_{\mathrm{name}}$, $\tau_s$, $\tau_{\mathrm{name},b}$, $\tau_{sb}$, $\tau_d$, probit on/off; $\tau_\mu=\XpTauMu$), nested selection (for each outer fold, the best setting on the other four folds' config-A test sets), the primary outcome and the ship rule before any outcome was computed: ship iff (a) the config-A ALC difference to our earlier predictor as deployed (S0) has a CI below zero, (b) the real testbed (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is not significantly worse, (c) no other configuration is significantly worse, and (d) engineering gates (never raises, smoke test and validator pass, a 62,000-label checkpoint in $\le$60 s). The selected setting lay on the grid boundary in $\tau_b$ and $\tau_d$; an addendum written after the primary result (\XpAddendumConsistent) added configuration E and an extended grid of \XpGridExtN{} settings, selected on A and E jointly; it returned \XpRobSameAsPrereg{} (Appendix~\ref{app:round4}). The stdlib \texttt{model.py} and the vectorised simulator agree to \XpEquivMaxDiff{} on \XpEquivN{} predictions.

\paragraph{Local testbeds.} The real testbed has \XpRealNPairs{} pairs: four small open models (Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B/3B/7B, Llama-3.2-3B) run locally with Ollama on GSM8K, SVAMP and a BIG-Bench-Hard mix under two harnesses (Appendix~\ref{app:olddata}); for round 4 all pairs share one global list. A synthetic IRT testbed was used in rounds 1--3 only.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Test-set composition is unknown.} The gain depends on how many pairs share a list and how they overlap; configurations A--E bracket plausible cases, and the gain is positive in all of them, but formative sets may be smaller or contain one pair per benchmark, where only the subject effect and the fallback-like pair shrinkage remain. \emph{Release structure.} Most release pairs are test-condition variants of one model; configuration E removes that structure and the gain persists, but the selected $\tau_d$ was tuned on the mixture. Item identity is exact text match within a benchmark; paraphrased or condition-specific item texts give no match. \emph{Pairs with fewer than 80 items} are handled under one assumed rule (D). \emph{Real testbed.} \XpRealNPairs{} pairs of small models on three benchmarks give wide CIs; there the gain over a correct per-pair filter is not significant, and XP is worse at $n=1$. \emph{Budget 0} still rests on the hand-set prior, whose weaknesses for frontier subjects are analysed in Appendix~\ref{app:frontier}. \emph{Acquisition} gains are small and mixed on the real testbed.

\section{Per-pair predictor (rounds 1--3; now the fallback and the budget-0 prior)}
\label{app:perpair}


All constants below are hand-set and are still shipped in \texttt{model.py}: the budget-0 prior is used by the cross-pair model, the rest is its fallback. The few whose values were adjusted after looking at the local testbeds are marked $^\dagger$ and have sensitivity analyses in Appendix~\ref{app:oldresults}.

\paragraph{Budget-0 prior.}
A logistic prior $p_0=\sigma(z)$ with $z=\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}+z_{\mathrm{item}},\pm\CPriorCap)$, so $p_0\in[\CPriorPLo,\CPriorPHi]$. The subject part is $z_{\mathrm{subj}}=\CSubjectShrink\cdot(\CYearSlope\,\mathrm{clip}(t-2024.5,\pm2) + e + \CSizeSlope\,\mathrm{clip}(\log_2(N/30\mathrm{B}),\pm4) + \CHarness\,\mathbb 1[\text{non-default harness}])$, where $t$ is the release year (fractional), $e\in\{0,\CEffortLow,\CEffortMid,\CEffortHigh\}$ encodes an empty, low, other or high \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, and $N$ is a parameter count parsed from the name (``7b'', ``70B''); when no count is present, name tokens such as \emph{mini/nano/flash} contribute \CSmallName{} and \emph{pro/opus/ultra} contribute \CLargeName. An intercept (\CIntercept) and a per-provider table (\CNProvider{} entries) complete the subject part; both are empty in the shipped prior and are the slots a fitted coefficient table would fill. The item part parses \texttt{key=value} pairs in \texttt{item\_features}: a tier/difficulty/level value matching \emph{hard/expert/\dots} adds \CTierHard, \emph{easy/basic/\dots} adds \CTierEasy, a numeric level $\ell\in[1,5]$ adds $-\CTierNum(\ell-3)$, and points above one subtract \CPoints{} each (capped at five); finally the item length adds $-\CLength\,\mathrm{clip}(\log_2(L/600),\pm3)$ for $L$ characters. The directions encode common knowledge (newer, larger, reasoning-enabled subjects do better; harder, longer, higher-value items are failed more often); the magnitudes were chosen to move the prior by at most a few tenths of a logit per attribute and were not tuned on either testbed.

\paragraph{Pair-level shrinkage.}
Given $n$ acquired labels $y_1,\dots,y_n$ of the same pair with item logits $z_{\mathrm{item}}^{(j)}$ and mean $\bar z$, the prior rate is $r=\sigma(\mathrm{clip}(z_{\mathrm{subj}}+\bar z))$ and the posterior mean of a Beta--binomial model with prior strength $\alpha_0=\CAlpha^\dagger$ pseudo-counts is $\rho=(\alpha_0 r+\sum_j y_j)/(\alpha_0+n)$~\citep{efron1975}. The pair estimate for a target item is $p_{\mathrm{pair}}=\sigma(\mathrm{logit}\,\rho + z_{\mathrm{item}}-\bar z)$, i.e.\ the item cues shift the pair rate on the logit scale.

\paragraph{Item-level component 1: ridge features.}
From $n\ge\CRidgeMinN$ labels, three cheap features of the item text---log length, digit density and log(1+number of sentences)---are standardised over the acquired items and a logistic regression with offset $\mathrm{logit}\,p_{\mathrm{pair}}$ and ridge penalty $\lambda=\CRidgeLambda$ is fitted by six Newton steps; its adjustment $\mathrm{clip}(\beta^\top \tilde f,\pm\CRidgeCap)$ is added to the target's logit.

\paragraph{Item-level component 2: TF-IDF nearest neighbours.}
Item texts (content, features and interactors, truncated to \CMaxTextChars{} characters) are tokenised into lower-case words and numbers; the acquired set defines the vocabulary and document frequencies, with sublinear term frequency, smoothed idf and $\ell_2$ normalisation (bigram weight \CBigramWeight; target terms absent from the acquired vocabulary are dropped: \CRestrictOov). With cosine similarities $s_j$ between the target and acquired item $j$ and weights $w_j=(\kappa s_j)^2$, the correction is a shrunken kernel-weighted residual,
\begin{equation}
p = \mathrm{clip}\Big(a + \frac{n}{n+N_0}\cdot\frac{\sum_j w_j\,(y_j-a)}{\tau+\sum_j w_j}\Big),\qquad N_0=\CKnnN,\ \tau=\CTau,
\end{equation}
where the anchor $a$ is the pair estimate (optionally including the ridge adjustment) and $\kappa=\CKappa$ by default. With at most 31 short documents most bigrams are unique, so cosine similarities are small (typically below 0.15 even for related items), which deliberately keeps the correction small unless items share many terms; Appendix~\ref{app:ablations} compares a sharper unigram-only representation.

\paragraph{Leave-one-out gating.}
From $n\ge\CLooMinN$ labels, every configuration in $\{\text{ridge off, on}\}\times\{\kappa\in\{0,\CKappas\}\}$ ($\kappa=0$ means no $k$NN) is scored by its leave-one-out Brier score on the acquired labels themselves: label $j$ is predicted from the other $n-1$ labels, including a leave-one-out shrinkage estimate and a leave-one-out ridge fit. The best configuration replaces plain shrinkage only if its mean per-item improvement exceeds \CLooSeMult{} standard error(s) of that mean$^\dagger$; otherwise the predictor returns $p_{\mathrm{pair}}$. This makes adaptation \emph{opt-in per pair and budget}: a pair whose labels show no exploitable structure is predicted by shrinkage alone. Below \CLooMinN{} labels no item-level component is used at all (in preliminary runs an always-on $k$NN from one or three labels only added noise at $B_1$ and $B_3$).

\paragraph{Acquisition policy.}
\texttt{labeling.py} implements the streaming interface and never raises. Candidates are accepted in stream order (uniformly random, hence the same label distribution as the evaluator's default) except that a candidate whose maximum TF-IDF cosine similarity to an already acquired item exceeds \CDedupSim{} is skipped while fewer than 90\% of the remaining candidates are needed; it always accepts when the remaining candidates are needed to spend the budget. We compare this \emph{novelty} filter with the evaluator's random policy, plain stream order, the organisers' uncertainty band $|p-0.5|\le0.15$~\citep{paiec_baselines_2026}, and a novelty-plus-uncertainty variant (skip $|p-0.5|>\CUncSkip$ once \CUncMinN{} labels are known).


\paragraph{Implementation (shipped files).} Predictions are clipped to $[\CClipLo,\CClipHi]$; \texttt{predict} is wrapped in a fallback chain (cross-pair model $\to$ per-pair full model $\to$ shrinkage only $\to$ smoothed label mean $\to$ 0.5), accepts labels given as booleans, numbers or strings, tolerates missing fields, malformed entries and non-dictionary inputs, and is deterministic. The submission ZIP (\ZipKB{} kB; members \ZipMembers) contains no \texttt{models.txt} and no \texttt{requirements.txt}: both files import only standard-library modules (\texttt{model.py}: \texttt{array}, \texttt{base64}, \texttt{hashlib}, \texttt{json}, \texttt{math}, \texttt{os}, \texttt{re}, \texttt{sys}, \texttt{threading}, \texttt{zlib}; the round-3 item-text-prior hooks stay inactive because no weight file is shipped; \texttt{labeling.py} loads its sibling \texttt{model.py} by path) and make no network calls. The organisers' \texttt{check\_submission\_zip.py} (commit \texttt{cc88a959}) \ValidatorStatus{} on the ZIP. Our smoke test calls \texttt{predict} with the organisers' sample inputs at every budget and with malformed labels and inputs (\SmokeNCases{} cases), plus \XpSmokeXpCases{} cross-pair cases, and checks determinism; with the cross-pair model enabled the mean latency is \SmokeMsShort{} ms per call on short items and \SmokeMsLong{} ms at budget 31 with 300-word items (maximum \SmokeMaxMsLong{} ms), about \SmokeCallsPerSec{} calls per second on one laptop core.

\paragraph{The training release: validation and a fit that was not shipped.} \texttt{fit\_prior\_from\_measurement\_db.py} builds one row per (subject, test condition, item) with the mean response over trials as label---\FitNPairs{} pairs with at least 20 distinct items from \FitNBench{} benchmarks, \FitNRows{} rows (pairs capped at \FitMaxItemsPerPair{} items; \FitRelNPairsAlc{} pairs have the 80 items the protocol requires)---and does two things (Appendix~\ref{app:fit}). First, it evaluates the rounds 1--3 prior-plus-shrinkage rule with the protocol in closed form (50/50 split, 31 random acquisitions, Eq.~\ref{eq:alc}; without the item-level components, which change the ALC by $<0.001$ locally): ALC \FitRelHandAlc{} [\FitRelHandAlcLo, \FitRelHandAlcHi] against \FitRelEmpAlc{} [\FitRelEmpAlcLo, \FitRelEmpAlcHi] for the organisers' empirical mean and \FitRelConstAlc{} for the constant, with $\alpha_0=4$ the best of $\{2,4,8\}$. The ordering and the size of the gain measured locally thus transfer to real systems on real benchmarks. Second, it fits an $L_2$-penalised logistic regression on exactly the feature terms of the hand-set prior plus a provider table and benchmark fixed effects (nuisance parameters; the shipped intercept is their mean), pairs weighted equally, with five-fold cross-validation over whole benchmarks. The fitted prior \emph{loses} to the hand-set one on held-out benchmarks at every penalty (budget-0 Brier \FitCMinBZero{}--\FitCMaxBZero{} against \FitRelHandBZero; paired differences at the selected setting \FitPairBZeroMean{} [\FitPairBZeroLo, \FitPairBZeroHi] at budget 0 and \FitPairAlcMean{} [\FitPairAlcLo, \FitPairAlcHi] in ALC), and an intercept-only prior is worse still (\FitRelIntOnlyBZero). The ship-only-if-better rule therefore kept the hand-set constants; in rounds 1--3 no coefficient of the submission derived from the release (round 4 selects the cross-pair model's prior standard deviations on it, Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Where the data speak, the fitted signs agree with the hand-set values; what held-out benchmarks punish is the provider table (Appendix~\ref{app:fit}).

\paragraph{An item-text difficulty prior (pre-registered; not shipped).} Benchmark difficulty dominates the pair rate (benchmark means have standard deviation \FitBenchRateSd) and the attribute prior cannot see it. We therefore tested, with design and ship rule fixed in \texttt{PREREG.md} (SHA-256 \texttt{\TpPreregSha}\dots) before any fit, whether the item text predicts difficulty on \emph{unseen} benchmarks: a ridge regression on \TpBits{} signed-hashed buckets (words, bigrams, character trigrams, \texttt{item\_features}) and 16 text statistics (length, \LaTeX, code, answer choices, \dots), trained on the item logit the hand-set prior leaves unexplained (\TpNTargetItems{} items, benchmarks weighted equally) and stacked onto the prior as $T=Ws+B$ with $W,B$ fitted on out-of-fold scores; shrinkage is unchanged. On the same benchmark-held-out folds, every choice nested inside the training benchmarks, the ALC changes by \TpDiffAlcMean{} [\TpDiffAlcLo, \TpDiffAlcHi] (cluster bootstrap over \TpNClusters{} benchmarks), closing \TpGapZero\%, \TpGapOne\% and \TpGapThree\% of the gap to the pair-rate oracle at $n=0,1,3$. The inner CV chose the largest penalty of the grid in \TpNFoldsAlphaMax{} of \TpNFolds{} folds and the out-of-fold slope $W$ was \emph{negative} in all \TpNFoldsNegW{} (\TpFoldWMin{} to \TpFoldWMax): text patterns learned on some benchmarks point the wrong way on others. Exploratory variants registered afterwards (penalties up to $10^4$; statistics alone) give \TpEOneDiffMean{} and \TpETwoDiffMean, the local testbeds \TpRPairFullMean{} (real, \TpRPairFullSig) and \TpSPairFullMean{} (synthetic, \TpSPairFullSig). Headroom exists---knowing each held-out benchmark's mean rate would gain \TpDiffBenchOracleMean{} ALC---but item text in this form does not reach it; the rule fails and the submission is unchanged (Appendix~\ref{app:textprior}).

\paragraph{Real Ollama testbed.}
To obtain item-level correctness labels of real language models we ran four small open-weight models locally with Ollama (Q4\_K\_M quantisation, greedy decoding, CPU only): Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B, 3B and 7B and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Three public benchmarks were used, none of which appears in the PAIEC inventory (only a derived variant, \texttt{mr\_gsm8k}, does): the first 200 GSM8K test items, the first 200 SVAMP test items (with the SVAMP problem type as \texttt{item\_features}), and a 300-item mix of ten BIG-Bench-Hard subtasks (30 items each, subtask name as \texttt{item\_features}). Two harnesses are represented: a direct-answer prompt (``reply with only the final answer''; exact match after numeric or option normalisation) and, for GSM8K/SVAMP, an earlier 8-shot chain-of-thought run of the Qwen models whose greedy answers we re-used. Each (model, harness, benchmark) triple with at least 80 items is one pair, giving \RNPairsMeta{} pairs, \RNItemsTotal{} labelled items, and success rates from \RAccMin\% to \RAccMax\% (Table~\ref{tab:testbed} in the appendix). The subject dictionaries carry the real model names, providers, release dates and harness names, so the budget-0 prior sees realistic inputs; the raw records are released with the code.

\paragraph{Synthetic IRT testbed.}
Because the real testbed is small (small models, three benchmarks), we complement it with a generator that follows a two-parameter item response model~\citep{lord1980,embretson2000}. Subject $s$ has ability $\theta_s$ correlated with its visible attributes (release year, reasoning effort, parameter count) plus noise; benchmark $b$ has a location $\mu_b$, a discrimination $a_b$ and $K=5$ topic clusters with difficulty offsets $c_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}^2)$; item $i$ in cluster $k(i)$ has difficulty $b_i=\mu_b+c_{k(i)}+\gamma\,z_{\mathrm{len}}(i)+\varepsilon_i$ and $\Pr(y_{si}=1)=\sigma(a_b(\theta_s-b_i))$. Item texts mix cluster-specific vocabulary (35\% of tokens), benchmark template words, ambient vocabulary and numbers, so that lexical similarity carries difficulty information exactly when $\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}>0$; half of the benchmarks expose a \texttt{tier=easy/medium/hard} feature derived from difficulty terciles. The main setting uses \SNSubjects{} subjects $\times$ \SNBenchmarks{} benchmarks $=$ \SNPairs{} pairs with 100--240 items each, $\sigma_{\mathrm{topic}}=\SSigma$ and $\gamma=\SGamma$; a sweep varies both. Because the true probabilities are known, we can report two oracle references: the Brier score of the true item probabilities (the floor of any predictor) and that of the evaluation-set success rate used as a constant (the floor of any predictor without item-level information).


\paragraph{Experimental setup.} Our rounds 1--3 harness reproduces the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:task} with \emph{per-pair} label lists (round 4 replaces this by the global list, Section~\ref{sec:protocol}): items of each pair are split 50/50 with a seeded shuffle, the acquisition pool is streamed in a seeded random order, the policy under test selects up to 31 labels, and the nested prefixes of that one trajectory are passed to every predictor for every evaluation item, so all methods are compared on identical labels. The evaluator's default policy is reproduced as accept-with-probability $\text{labels\_remaining}/\text{items\_remaining}$. We report Brier ALC with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals over pairs (\NBoot{} resamples), paired bootstrap differences between methods (same pairs, same trajectories), per-budget Brier scores and pooled ECE. Baselines: the constant 0.5; the organisers' empirical mean (their code: 0.5 without matching labels, the raw mean otherwise); our prior alone; shrinkage alone; shrinkage with each item-level component always on; the LOO-gated model (shipped in rounds 1--3).

\paragraph{Headroom.}
The oracle rows explain why. On the synthetic testbed, knowing the true probability of every item gives an ALC of \SOrPAlc, knowing only the evaluation-set success rate \SOrRateAlc: perfect item-level discrimination is worth \SHeadroomItem{} ALC, to be recovered from 15--31 labels at budgets that carry 30\% of the weight. Shrinkage (\SShrinkAlc) is \SHeadroomRate{} above the rate oracle; that gap is the price of estimating a rate from few labels, concentrated at $n\le 3$ ($B_1$: \SHeadroomRateBOne; $B_{31}$: \SHeadroomRateBThirtyOne), and only a better prior, i.e.\ training data, can reduce it. On the real testbed the gap is \RHeadroomRate{} (\RShrinkAlc{} vs.\ \ROrRateAlc). The two parts of the headroom are of similar size but call for different remedies: a prior fitted to real subjects and benchmarks, and item difficulty information from outside the pair.

\paragraph{Gating.}
Replacing the LOO gate by always-on components at their default settings gives \SLooAlwaysFullAlc{} versus \SLooGatedFullAlc{} (synthetic; paired gated$-$always \SLooPairGatedAlwaysMean{} [\SLooPairGatedAlwaysLo, \SLooPairGatedAlwaysHi], \SLooPairGatedAlwaysSig) and \RLooAlwaysFullAlc{} versus \RLooGatedFullAlc{} (real; \RLooPairGatedAlwaysMean{} [\RLooPairGatedAlwaysLo, \RLooPairGatedAlwaysHi], \RLooPairGatedAlwaysSig). The gate pays where always-on components overfit (the synthetic ridge) and costs a little where a component carries real signal (on the real testbed longer GSM8K problems are failed more often, which the always-on ridge exploits at $n\ge 15$); the second effect is within noise. Requiring 0, 1 or 2 standard errors of LOO improvement gives full-model ALCs of \SLooMultZeroFullAlc, \SLooMultOneFullAlc{} and \SLooMultTwoFullAlc{} (synthetic) and \RLooMultZeroFullAlc, \RLooMultOneFullAlc{} and \RLooMultTwoFullAlc{} (real); we ship the most conservative setting.

\paragraph{Sentence embeddings.}
The embeddings were computed offline for the real testbed (the model is on the validator's allow-list via \texttt{models.txt}, so it could have been shipped). They change the full model's ALC from \REmbTfidfFullAlc{} to \REmbMinilmFullAlc{} (paired \REmbPairFullMean{} [\REmbPairFullLo, \REmbPairFullHi], \REmbPairFullSig) and the always-on $k$NN variant from \REmbTfidfKnnAlc{} to \REmbMinilmKnnAlc{} (\REmbPairKnnMean{} [\REmbPairKnnLo, \REmbPairKnnHi], \REmbPairKnnSig), at the cost of a neural dependency, model loading and about 10~ms per embedding on CPU.

\paragraph{Sharper lexical similarity does not help.}
A unigram-only TF-IDF whose target terms are restricted to the acquired vocabulary separates same-topic from other-topic synthetic items far better (mean cosine about 0.3 versus 0.1 in the generator) but makes the always-on $k$NN \emph{worse}: paired against the shipped representation, the always-on $k$NN changes by \STfidfUniKnnVsShippedMean{} [\STfidfUniKnnVsShippedLo, \STfidfUniKnnVsShippedHi] (synthetic) and \RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedMean{} [\RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedLo, \RTfidfUniKnnVsShippedHi] (real), and the gated full model by \STfidfUniFullVsShippedMean{} [\STfidfUniFullVsShippedLo, \STfidfUniFullVsShippedHi] and \RTfidfUniFullVsShippedMean{} [\RTfidfUniFullVsShippedLo, \RTfidfUniFullVsShippedHi]. With a handful of neighbours per topic the kernel-weighted residual is dominated by label noise; the bigram-diluted similarity acts as a regulariser. Item-level signal exists (the oracles show it) but 31 labels are too few to exploit it through similarity alone.

\section{Fitting pipeline details}
\label{app:fit}

\texttt{fit\_prior\_from\_measurement\_db.py} runs in five steps, each a sub-command. \emph{plan}: list the repository, download the small \texttt{benchmarks} table of every directory and keep those with \texttt{response\_type = binary} and \texttt{granularity = item} (the organisers' eligibility rule in \texttt{tools/prepare\_data.py}); record the bytes still to download and stop above a cap (30 GB by default). \emph{download}: fetch the eligible \texttt{subjects}, \texttt{items} and \texttt{responses} tables into the organisers' payload layout. \emph{fit}: build one row per (subject, test condition, item) with the mean response over trials as label (fractional labels enter as two weighted rows), each pair receiving total weight one; compute the subject and item terms with the shipped \texttt{model.subject\_terms} and \texttt{model.item\_terms}, so that fitted coefficients map one-to-one onto the constants of Appendix~\ref{app:perpair}; add a one-hot for providers with at least 15 pairs and one for each training benchmark; fit an $L_2$-penalised logistic regression. Five-fold cross-validation holds out whole benchmarks; the penalty $C$ and the logit cap are selected by the pair-weighted budget-0 Brier score of the prior on held-out pairs, and $\alpha_0\in\{2,4,8\}$ by the closed-form shrinkage ALC of the protocol (50/50 split, 31 random acquisitions, Eq.~\ref{eq:alc}) on held-out pairs with at least 80 items, paired against the hand-set prior on the same pairs. \emph{testbeds}: evaluate the fitted table inside the harness on both local testbeds against the shipped constants. \emph{dry-run}: generate tables with the release schema (shared pool of subjects, random subsets per benchmark, benchmark difficulty offsets with standard deviation one logit, labels from a known coefficient table over the prior's own terms, two ineligible benchmarks) and run \emph{fit} on them. The dry run used \DryNBench{} eligible benchmarks, \DryNPairs{} pairs and \DryNRows{} rows; a light-regularisation fit recovers all \DryNSlopes{} slope signs with maximum error \DryMaxAbsErr{} and mean error \DryMeanAbsErr{} logits, and the fitted benchmark effects have standard deviation \DryBenchSd. Its cross-validated selection chose $C=\DrySelC$ and $\alpha_0=\DrySelAlpha$ and did not beat the hand-set prior (budget-0 Brier \DryPairBZeroMean{} [\DryPairBZeroLo, \DryPairBZeroHi]; shrinkage ALC \DryPairAlcMean{} [\DryPairAlcLo, \DryPairAlcHi]), so on these tables the rule would have kept the hand-set constants.

\paragraph{The run on the release.} Revision \texttt{\FitRevisionShort}; \FitNEligible{} eligible directories, \FitNBench{} with at least one pair of 20 or more distinct items; the pair unit is (benchmark directory, subject, test condition), since the release records several test conditions per subject on many benchmarks; \FitNPairs{} pairs, \FitNRows{} rows (each pair capped at \FitMaxItemsPerPair{} items); \FitNProviders{} providers with at least 15 pairs received a coefficient. Pair success rates have mean \FitRateMean{} and standard deviation \FitRateSd{} across pairs (benchmark means: standard deviation \FitBenchRateSd), while the hand-set $p_0$ has mean \FitHandPMean{} and standard deviation \FitHandPSd. The penalty grid ran from $C=\FitCMin$ to \FitCMax; the selection took $C=\FitSelC$, cap \FitSelCap{} and $\alpha_0=\FitSelAlpha$ (cross-validated budget-0 Brier \FitSelBZero, shrinkage ALC \FitSelAlc). The fitted benchmark effects have standard deviation \FitBenchEffectSd{} logits. Fitted vs.\ effective hand-set coefficients: release year \FitCoefYear{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefYear{} per year, size \FitCoefSize{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefSize{} per doubling, hard-tier cue \FitCoefTierHard{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefTierHard, easy-tier cue \FitCoefTierEasy{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefTierEasy, item length \FitCoefLength{} vs.\ \HandEffCoefLength; the reasoning-effort and harness terms shrink to about zero (\FitCoefEffortHigh, \FitCoefHarness; \FitFracPairsNoProvider{} of pairs carry no provider field and most carry no effort or harness field), and the provider table spans \FitProvMin{} (\texttt{\FitProvMinName}) to \FitProvMax{} (\texttt{\FitProvMaxName}). Inside the harness on the local testbeds the fitted table changes the full model's ALC by \FitRealPairFullMean{} [\FitRealPairFullLo, \FitRealPairFullHi] (real) and \FitSynPairFullMean{} [\FitSynPairFullLo, \FitSynPairFullHi] (synthetic); the result files are \texttt{prior\_fit.json} and \texttt{prior\_fit\_testbeds.json}, and the ship rule's verdict is recorded there (\FitShip).

\section{Item-text difficulty prior: details}
\label{app:textprior}

\paragraph{Pre-registration and outcome.} \texttt{experiments/results/round3/PREREG.md} (SHA-256 prefix \texttt{\TpPreregSha}, recorded in every result file and \TpPreregConsistent{} at build time) fixed features, target, model, nested selection, evaluation and ship rule before any text model was fitted. \texttt{PREREG\_ADDENDUM\_1.md} (\TpAddendumConsistent) registered the two exploratory variants after the primary folds had run, explicitly without influence on the decision. The rule: ship iff (a) the benchmark-held-out ALC difference (text minus hand-set) has a 95\% cluster-bootstrap CI below zero, (b) the real testbed is not significantly worse, and the engineering gates hold ($\ge200$ calls/s on one core with a new target item per call, weights $\le5$~MB, validator and smoke test pass, never raises). Outcome: (a) \TpRuleA, (b) \TpRuleB, gates \TpGates; the text prior is \TpShip.

\paragraph{Features and target.} JSON contents (the release stores many items as JSON objects) are flattened into \texttt{key: value} lines; words and word bigrams come from the first 2,000 characters (digit runs mapped to a length token), character trigrams from the first 500, and \texttt{item\_features} tokens form a fourth family; each family is sublinear-tf weighted and $\ell_2$-normalised, then hashed with \texttt{zlib.crc32} (sign from bit 31) into \TpBits{} buckets. The target of item $i$ is $\mathrm{logit}\,\frac{n_ir_i+0.5}{n_i+1}$ minus the mean hand-set subject logit of the $n_i$ pairs that evaluated it and minus its hand-set item logit, clipped to $\pm5$. At most 1,000 items per benchmark (\TpNTargetItems{} in total) are used for fitting; all \TpNItems{} items of the frame are scored (\TpNnz{} non-zeros). Exact duplicates of a test item's content in a training benchmark are rare (\TpDupMean\% of frame items on average), so shared items do not inflate the cross-validation.

\paragraph{Nested selection and fits.} In each outer fold a four-fold benchmark-grouped inner CV chooses the ridge penalty from $\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3,\TpAlphaGridMax\}$ by weighted MSE of the target; the inner out-of-fold scores give $W,B$ (pair-weighted logistic likelihood), the cap ($\{1.5,2.5,3.5\}$, training budget-0 Brier) and the within-pair weight $\lambda$ of the text offset ($\{0,0.5,1\}$, training closed-form ALC). Selected: penalty \TpFoldAlphas{} in every fold (the grid maximum), caps \TpFoldCaps, $\lambda=\TpFoldLambdas$, $W$ from \TpFoldWMin{} to \TpFoldWMax{} and $B$ from \TpFoldBMin{} to \TpFoldBMax. The held-out $R^2$ of the residual target was \TpFoldRsqMin{} to \TpFoldRsqMax{} across folds (inner CV: \TpInnerRsqMin{} to \TpInnerRsqMax). The final fit on all \TpNBench{} benchmarks again selected penalty \TpFinalAlpha{} with $W=\TpFinalW$, $B=\TpFinalB$, cap \TpFinalCap{} and $\lambda=\TpFinalLambda$; it is kept as \texttt{text\_prior\_candidate.json} (\TpFinalMB{} MB) and not shipped.
